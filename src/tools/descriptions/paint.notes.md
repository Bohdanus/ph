# Paint family — research notes

The seven tools whose work is putting pixels on, taking them off, or moving them: `brush`, `pencil`, `color-replacement`, `eraser`, `magic-eraser`, `paint-bucket`, `clone-stamp`.

**How to read the tables.** `Status` is the app's fidelity for that one Photoshop control, checked against **both** `src/tools/settings.ts` and the tool's own component under `src/components/ToolbarsRight/` before anything was called `missing`. `present` renders and behaves; `partial` renders but diverges; `missing` renders nothing; `inert` renders with Photoshop's own label but writes nothing. `appRef` points at the **wiring**, not at the label.

**On the currency of the citations, precisely, because an earlier version of this sentence was a false claim rather than a stale one.** It read "every line number in this file and in `paint.ts` was re-derived against this worktree from the meaning of its claim", and the numbers refuted it: it cited `settings.ts:126` and `settings.ts:143` for the Brush's and the Pencil's `alpha: 0.9` (real lines 131 and 148), `brush.ts:23` for `PAINT_BLEND_MODES` (real line 31), and `toolbarPlaceholderConfig.ts:4` plus `ToolbarPlaceholder.tsx:25-32` for a Color Replacement placeholder bar that no longer exists. **What is true now: the citations this pass rewrote were each re-derived by opening the line and confirming it holds the claim made of it, and the ones this pass did not rewrite were each re-read and left alone only because they still hold.** No bar in this family is a `PlaceholderTool` any more, so the `inert` caveat about chip labels that do not carry their own control's name applies to no row in this file.

**Version.** Photoshop 2024–2026 throughout. Two version boundaries are load-bearing and are named where they occur: `Smoothing` and `Symmetry` are 2017/18-era additions, and the option-bar `Hardness` control is gone from the bars of the tools that select their tip through the Brush Preset picker — the Brush, the Pencil and the Clone Stamp. It is **not** gone everywhere: Adobe's current Eraser page still says "In Brush and Pencil modes, adjust Size, Hardness, Opacity, and Flow from the Options bar", so the Eraser keeps both on its bar, which is why the Brush and Pencil rows read as an inconsistency until that sentence is in the same file as them.

**Retrieval boundary.** Adobe's `helpx.adobe.com` pages were read through the live search crawler this pass, which is the only route that returns their text; a direct fetch of one Adobe-hosted mirror timed out and is not evidence of anything either way. Two Adobe manuals are cited through mirrors of Adobe's own text (`manualsdir.com`, `adobe.manymanuals.com`, and a Wacom-hosted PDF of the CC 2018 help), and one — the blend-mode descriptions page — was confirmed through a photographic club's PDF of that page, which reproduces Adobe's current wording including the "– Brush blend mode" suffixes. Each is labelled as a mirror where it is load-bearing, because the mirror is not Adobe but the words are Adobe's.

## Brush Tool

Photoshop's general painting tool. Click and drag to lay down a stroke of the foreground color on the active layer.

**Load-bearing distinction — Opacity is not Flow. Adobe now states it, and this file previously said it did not.** Opacity is a hard ceiling for one stroke: the stroke cannot exceed the set level however many times the pointer crosses the same pixels, and only a new stroke re-applies it. Flow is the per-dab build-up _inside_ that ceiling. **An earlier version of this paragraph opened "No Adobe page states an accumulation formula for either setting" and that sentence is now falsified.** `painting-tools.html` gives the ceiling — "As you paint over an area, the opacity does not exceed the set level no matter how many times you move the pointer over the area, until you release the mouse button. If you stroke over the area again, you apply additional color, equivalent to the set opacity" — and then a worked example of the build-up, not just its existence: "if you set the opacity to 33% and the flow to 33%, each time you move over an area, its color moves 33% toward the brush color. The total will not exceed 33% opacity unless you release the mouse button and stroke over the area again." That is the accumulate-under-a-clamp model, from Adobe, with numbers: each pass moves a fixed fraction of the remaining gap and the sequence converges to the Opacity. **What is still not Adobe's is the closed form.** The single expression that names the ceiling directly is the "over" composition of the two rates, `ps = flow + op − flow·op`, which is why it appears so often; Adobe's 33/33 example states the iteration, not that expression, and nothing in `paint.ts` asserts the expression.

Krita's manual documents the same ceiling semantics from the other side, having changed flow and opacity from "add up to one another" to multiplying in 4.2 "so in 4.2 this finally got corrected to the flow and opacity multiplying, resulting in much more subtle strokes" — an independent application that treats Opacity as a maximum and Flow as a per-dab rate, which is the pair `paint.ts` asserts. Adobe's own example is consistent with it: 33% flow at 33% opacity, iterated, lands on 33% and stops.

This is the single most commonly mis-implemented pair in a paint engine, and it is why `Flow` is `missing` for the app's Brush rather than `partial`.

**The pair is sourced, and the third-party pages below are corroboration rather than the citation.** Two Adobe pages now carry the wording and both are in `sources`. What follows is retained because it states the ceiling in one sentence each and is worth keeping readable, and because it is the corpus a reviewer will reach for — but each is a third party and neither is what the claim now rests on. asktimgrey.com's _Flow versus Opacity_ states the cap and the clamp in one sentence — "If you paint back and forth without releasing the mouse, you'll build up the effect in all areas that you paint over more than once, but that build up will **never exceed 50% strength based on the Opacity setting**" — and, separately, that at Opacity 50 with Flow 100 you "keep the mouse button held down and paint back and forth over the same area, you'll still only be painting with 50% gray, without having the painting effect building up based on passing over the same area multiple times." It also states the re-application half, which the data file's "a new stroke re-applies it" rests on: "Even with the Opacity set to a reduced value, however, if you release the mouse and then paint over the same area again, the effect will build up." photoshopcafe.com's _Should you use Flow or Opacity_ says the same in the demonstration: "No matter how hard I press, I'm going to get 50 percent… It's not going to go more than 50 percent," and then, lifting the pen, "I can do another 50 percent."

**The Bar Has No Top-Bar Size, and that is a version fact the file previously got backwards for two of the three tools it applied it to.** Current Photoshop's Brush bar carries no top-bar `Size` and no top-bar `Hardness`; both are set inside the Brush Preset picker popout. Adobe's own words, from `painting-tools.html`: "You can quickly choose presets from the Brush Preset picker in the options bar, which lets you temporarily modify the size and hardness of a brush preset," and the Brush-presets page adds that such a change is "temporary if you modify a preset brush, such as changing its size or hardness." The retouch page states the same rule for that family: "Set the brush's Size, Hardness, and Spacing from the brush options dropdown menu in the Options bar." **The Clone Stamp row already said this and the Brush and Pencil rows did not, which is the inconsistency that was worth fixing** — not because the Eraser contradicts it, but because the Eraser legitimately keeps both on its bar, so a reader had no way to tell which rule applied where. The 1–5000 figure is unchanged and now lives in the `Brush Preset Picker` detail, where Adobe puts it.

**Bar order is Tool Preset Picker, then Brush Preset Picker, then the rest.** The Tool Preset Picker is first because Adobe places it by position and not by name: "You can also select the Tool Presets dropdown menu next to the tool at the upper-left of the Options bar." The Brush Preset Picker is the leftmost of the bar's own controls once that one is set aside, which is how the `Size` detail came to be folded into it.

**Mode is a layer property, not a stroke property.** The app seeds the created layer's `blendMode` from the tool args. That is a defensible simplification for a Normal-mode brush and a visible divergence for the other fifteen.

**The blend-mode list was wrong in two ways, and both are the kind of error a count hides.** The old text said the Brush offers "the 27 layer blend modes plus Dissolve, Behind and Clear", which counts Dissolve twice — **Dissolve is one of the 27**, as Adobe's own `BlendMode` enum shows (`DISSOLVE` in the list at the UXP constants reference, alongside `PASSTHROUGH` and the 25 others). And the availability is not uniform across the family: **Behind is the Brush's alone**, documented as working "only in layers with Lock Transparency deselected" and as a Brush blend mode, while **Clear goes to "the Shape tools (when fill region is selected), Paint Bucket tool, Brush tool, Pencil tool, Fill command, and Stroke command"**. So the Brush's list is the 27 plus two, the Pencil's and the Paint Bucket's are the 27 plus `Clear`, and the Clone Stamp's is the 27 with neither. The Brush's total is 29, not "thirty-plus" — the old `appNotes` said "thirty-plus" and that has been corrected to 29.

**The Tool Preset Picker is a current control, and a removal claim here was wrong.** Photoshop's bar opens with a **Tool Preset Picker** at the far left, beside the tool icon, whose menu carries `New Tool Preset`, `Rename Tool Preset`, `Delete Tool Preset`, `Reset Tool` and `Reset All Tools`. An earlier pass recorded this control as gone, on the strength of one secondary source: clippingpathca.com (2025) says Tool Presets "had their own dropdown menu at the far left of the Options bar" in "Photoshop CS6 or early CC versions" and that they are "now found inside the Brush Settings or Presets interface." **That is refuted by Adobe's own current page**, last updated 2026-02-23, which states: "You can also select the Tool Presets dropdown menu next to the tool at the upper-left of the Options bar," with a Create New Tool Preset icon on it. Julieanne Kost's Adobe blog independently gives the same two access routes and the right-click `Reset Tool` / `Reset All Tools` items. A blog post is not a sufficient authority for an absence, and here it was not even right.

So the row is `missing` because the **app** does not render the control, not because Photoshop has dropped it — and the Brush, Pencil and Clone Stamp rows all say so explicitly, with Adobe's sentence quoted. The app's `Brush Preset` select — three synthetic settings snapshots plus a `Custom` row, wired at `Paint.tsx:123` — is the app's own addition. That is unaffected: it is not standing in for a control Photoshop renders, because it applies options-bar state in a position Photoshop's own picker also occupies while offering no picker of its own.

**There is no `Size` row, and that is a finding rather than an omission.** Photoshop's own `Size` is a real control and its 1–5000 range is correct; current Photoshop just does not put it on this bar. The app does, which is a divergence from Photoshop's bar and not from Photoshop's control, so it is recorded in the `Brush Preset Picker` detail (where Adobe puts it) and in `appNotes` (where the app's own addition belongs), and it is deliberately absent from this table — `types.ts` reserves `options[]` for controls Photoshop places in the bar. The app's slider is `Paint.tsx:105` and caps at `brush.ts:70-71`.

**`Smoothing` is not the only control these three bars share, and the old note here said it was.** The Brush `Smoothing` detail claimed to be "the only control on the bar the Pencil and the Eraser share with the Brush", which is false on two counts: Opacity and Symmetry are on all three bars too. Adobe's symmetry page scopes the butterfly button to "the Paint Brush, Pencil, and Eraser tools" and the Eraser page keeps Opacity on the bar (withdrawing it only in Block mode), so the honest statement is that three controls carry across, and that is what the detail now says.

**Smoothing: same name, different algorithm.** The app pulls every interior sample toward the midpoint of its neighbours by `smoothing/100` (`brush.ts:109-127`, consumed at `util/draw.ts:96`), which is a point-space filter. Photoshop's is a trailing anchor over the **raw** input stream at input rate, which is what makes Stroke Catch-up, Catch-up on Stroke End and Pulled String Mode expressible and what couples smoothing to zoom. The app's version is recognizably the same feature and not the same mechanism — hence `partial`, not `missing`.

**What matches.** One history state per stroke: the commit is the pointer-up (`DrawToolArea.tsx:143`). Opacity as a per-stroke ceiling. Smoothing at Photoshop's own 10% default (`brush.ts:74`).

**What does not match, and is easy to miss: the app's default Opacity is 90, not 100.** `settings.ts:131` seeds the brush's `alpha: 0.9`, and `settings.ts:148` does the same for the pencil, so a fresh document opens with the bar reading 90% where Photoshop's reads 100%. The control is still `present` and not `partial`: a wrong default is a default, not a divergence in what the control does, and the per-stroke-ceiling behaviour itself is exactly right. `types.ts:44-46` requires the difference to be written down rather than left for a reader to discover, so the `Opacity` rows in `paint.ts` carry it in `detail` and both `appNotes` repeat it. The Brush's own three synthetic presets carry their own `alpha` values and overwrite the seed on selection, so 90% is what a user sees on open, not an invariant.

**The largest divergence is not on the bar.** A brush stroke is committed by `createLayerFromTool` — it creates a **new layer** rather than painting the active one. Photoshop's Brush paints into the active layer. Nothing in the options table shows this.

**Modifiers and shortcuts, and the app binds almost none of them.** `Alt` temporarily switches to the Eyedropper; `Shift` constrains the stroke to a straight line between a click and a Shift-click. `B` selects the tool, `[`/`]` change the tip size, and `0`–`9` set Opacity in tens. **All of those are Photoshop's, and `B` is the only one this app binds.** `AppKeyboardShortcuts.tsx` is the complete global table, and it registers no bracket key, no digit key, and no Alt or Shift override for any paint tool: every `shiftKey` in it belongs to a tool-cycle group, of which the brush group is the one at `:18` that also reaches the pencil and the color replacement. Outside that file, `altKey` is read in a pointer handler only by the shape, the magnetic lasso, the elliptical selection and the clone stamp, and `DrawToolArea.tsx` reads no modifier at all. So the `[`/`]`/`0`/`1` shortcut rows and the `Alt`/`Shift` modifier rows describe Photoshop and would be false as a description of this app, which is why `types.ts` treats the modifiers and shortcuts as the tool's Photoshop bindings and every divergence belongs in `appNotes`: both `appNotes` now say so in as many words. Note that the clone stamp's `Alt` row is the opposite case and is genuinely implemented, which is what makes the brush and pencil rows worth stating rather than leaving tacit.

**Airbrush reverses the digit binding, and Adobe's own document says so.** The `Airbrush` row claims that with the toggle on the number keys drive Flow and `Shift` with them drives Opacity. That was previously recorded as unverified, and a reviewer's counter-example pointed the other way — photoshopcafe.com's Flow/Opacity page says "the shift key will enable us to do the same thing with the shortcuts, but for Flow. So Shift five will give us 50% flow." That is not a contradiction, it is the **non**-Airbrush case: that page is describing the plain tool, and Adobe's CS3 shortcuts sheet states the reversal explicitly and names the exception. Verbatim: "To change the focus from Opacity to Flow (on the options bar) add the Shift key. Note, when the Airbrush attribute is assigned to a tool, then the tapping a number changes the Flow by default (adding the Shift key changes the Opacity)." The data file's sentence is Adobe's sentence. What the reviewer's quote does establish is that the two behaviours are routinely conflated by second-hand sources, which is worth knowing; the CS3 sheet's version is recorded in `## Unverified` as the open item, not the binding itself.

**`Protect Texture` and `Protect Tones` are two different controls, and the resemblance trips readers.** `Protect Texture` is a checkbox in the **Brush Settings panel**, and it belongs to the Brush's tip model. `Protect Tones` is a checkbox on the **Dodge/Burn group's** options bar, and it is not on the Brush's bar at all. Nothing here is inconsistent: the Brush Settings panel's last entry is `Protect Texture` and no row in `paint.ts` names `Protect Tones`, because the Dodge/Burn tools are not part of this file.

## Pencil Tool

A Brush whose tip is always hard-edged. Adobe's own one-line distinction: "The Brush tool creates soft strokes of color. The Pencil tool creates hard-edged lines."

**The hard edge is not antialiasing.** Two independent sources state it: the O'Reilly _Photoshop Elements_ Missing Manual — "it offers only hard-edged brushes. In other words, you can't draw fuzzy lines with the pencil"; and dummies.com, on the partial-sketch feature — "the edges of a pencil stroke can't even be antialiased." Ask Tim Grey's sharpening is that this is what creates the jagged stair-step diagonal: a round tip at 100% hardness with no antialiasing rasterizes as a chain of square pixels. That is also why the Pencil's default tip is the 1-pixel brush (dummies.com), and why the missing control most people would name first is not a rendering bug but a tip-model one.

**Auto Erase is decided once, at mouse-down.** Adobe's exact wording, from the CS3 manual: "If the center of the cursor is over the foreground color when you begin dragging, the area is erased to the background color. If the center of the cursor is over an area that doesn't contain the foreground color when you begin dragging, the area is painted with the foreground color." Crossing the other color mid-stroke does not switch modes. This is the only genuinely Pencil-specific feature, and it is `missing` in the app for a structural reason worth stating plainly: **the app has no background color in its tool state**, so the checkbox has nothing to choose between.

**Bar order matches the Brush's, and the same two corrections apply.** The Tool Preset Picker is at the upper left, so it is index 0 and the Brush Preset Picker follows it; there is no top-bar `Size`, and the 1–5000 figure folds into the picker's detail. `Mode` is the 27 layer blend modes plus `Clear` only — `Behind` is the Brush's alone.

**Flow is absent from the Pencil's bar entirely** — not listed above because Adobe's current Pencil bar does not carry it, which Ask Tim Grey states directly ("The Pencil tool also lacks the Flow setting on the Options bar"). That is a Photoshop fact the app happens to match, and the one place the app is not behind.

**The hard edge here is real but by construction.** Both the Brush and the Pencil go through the same bar factory and the same single-`stroke()` painter with a round cap, so the two do agree on the one property that distinguishes them — and they agree for the wrong reason. The edge is hard because the canvas rasterizes a round cap that way, not because a hard tip was selected. There is no tip model that could be made soft, which is the same root cause as the Eraser's `disabled` Brush mode.

**Keyboard.** Photoshop binds the Pencil to `N` (O'Reilly and dummies.com both). The `Alt` and `Shift` modifier rows are Photoshop's and the app binds neither: `AppKeyboardShortcuts.tsx` is the complete global table and registers no Alt override and no Shift constraint for any paint tool, and `altKey` in a pointer handler is read only by the shape, the magnetic lasso, the elliptical selection and the clone stamp. Both `appNotes` say so, for the same reason the Brush's do.

## Color Replacement Tool

Substitutes one color for another in a stroke while keeping the luminosity and the texture underneath. Adobe describes the default options bar as one that "maintain[s] the luminosity and saturation of a color while replacing its hue."

**The whole tool is a blend mode.** Adobe: "Generally, you should keep the blending mode set to Color." That is why it is a replacement and not a paint — the mode transfers the source color's hue and chroma onto the destination's luminosity rather than compositing on top. `Hue`, `Saturation` and `Luminosity` transfer a different single component each. Remove the mode and the tool is a brush. The lead citation for this used to be a `helpx.adobe.com` URL that answers 403 to any fetch, which is not a citation; `sources` now leads with a page that is actually readable and carries the same claim — photoshopessentials.com's Color Replacement tutorial states "The default blend mode is Color," names the four modes as Hue, Saturation, Color and Luminosity, and gives each one's effect ("Color is the default blend mode and will change both the hue and saturation. The brightness will remain unchanged"). The Adobe CS4 manual mirror is retained for the "keep the blending mode set to Color" sentence, and its step sequence — brush tip, then Mode, then Sampling, Limits, Tolerance, Anti-aliased — is the bar order the table below uses.

**Sampling and Limits are two different questions.** Sampling decides _which_ color is the target: Continuous resamples under the crosshair on every move, Once locks the color of the first click for the whole stroke, Background Swatch targets the background color. Limits decides _how far_ the replacement spreads: Contiguous from the pixel under the pointer, Discontiguous anywhere under the pointer, Find Edges contiguous but stopping at strong shape edges. A tolerance slider then sets how similar a pixel must be to count. **The three lists are Adobe's; no page states which entry in either is the default**, so neither `Sampling` nor `Limits` carries a "(default)" marker in `paint.ts` and the table's Default column below reads "not stated" for both.

**Limits is about reach, not membership, and that is what makes the `Contiguous` half of `purpose` right.** The `purpose` text used to contrast the tool with the Paint Bucket as replacing "as you drag rather than flooding a whole connected region in one click", which implied it does not flood. It does: Adobe's own wording for Contiguous is "Replaces colors that are contiguous with the color immediately under the pointer", and the app's code is more explicit still — it gathers every colour-matching pixel the stroke reached and floods outward from each one, one `createFloodFillMask` call per region (`colorReplacement.ts:376-398`). **What differs from the bucket is the trigger, not the reach**: one click chooses the colour to replace, and with Contiguous the replacement still takes the whole connected region from that one click. Discontiguous is the setting that confines it to the pixels under the brush. The mirror sentence in the Paint Bucket's `purpose` was half-false in the same way and has been corrected to match.

**Version note on Tolerance.** Adobe's CS4 manual says "enter a percentage value (ranging from 0 to 255)," which is the same odd unit the bucket uses. The commonly-cited default of 30% comes from third parties (photoshopessentials.com), not from Adobe — see `## Unverified`.

**The bar is a real options bar, and every earlier status in this file was measured against a component that no longer exists.** The previous version of this section was built on `placeholderToolGroups['color-replacement']` and on the chip list in `toolbarPlaceholderConfig.ts`, and it claimed six rows were `inert` because six chips rendered. **Both of those are now gone**: `toolbarPlaceholderConfig.ts:13` is a comment saying the tool "used to sit here", and the tool's `toolboxComponent` is the real `ColorReplacement` component (`toolsMap.ts:117`). The six statuses that were derived from the chip list were re-derived against `ColorReplacement.tsx` and four of them moved, in both directions — three `missing` controls turned out to be live, and three `inert` controls turned out to be absent:

- **`Brush Preset Picker`, `Mode` and `Anti-aliased` are `missing`**, not `inert`. Nothing renders any of them. The old note's careful sub-claim — that the `Mode` chip read `Blend Mode` and the `Anti-aliased` chip read `Anti-alias`, so those two were `inert` on a nearly-correct label rather than on an exact one — described a card that has since been deleted, and it is withdrawn with the card.
- **`Size` is `partial`, `Opacity` is `present`.** Both are live sliders, `Size` capped at 200 px where the picker offers 5000, and `Opacity` writing `args.alpha`. Both were `missing` before, which was the more serious error of the two kinds: it told a reader the tool cannot be scaled or softened when it can.
- **`Limits` and `Tolerance` are `partial`.** Both are live and both diverge: the Limits list is two of Adobe's three entries with the second misspelled, and Tolerance is on a 0–100 scale against Adobe's documented 0–255. That is precisely what `partial` is for — wired, and wrong.
- **`Sampling` stays `inert`, but for the right reason.** The component does render a real `disabled` select, so `inert` is the honest status. What the old note got wrong was the reason: it said the chip named Adobe's control and nothing read it. In fact the select's options are `Current Layer` and `All Layers` — **neither of Adobe's Continuous / Once / Background Swatch**. The one control that decides _which colour is the target_ is therefore not merely unwired, it is wired to a different question entirely, and the app has no background color for Background Swatch to name.

**`Smoothness` is on this bar and is not a Photoshop control, so it is in `appNotes` and not in the table.** `ColorReplacement.tsx:130-144` renders a 0-50 slider called `Smoothness`, and the component's own comment says what it is — "Photoshop's Smoothness (Anti-aliased on older builds)". No Adobe page for this tool names a `Smoothness` control: the CS4 manual's step sequence ends at Anti-aliased, and the tool's option list in the manual and in the flylib transcription of the same manual both stop there. `types.ts` reserves `options[]` for controls Photoshop places in the bar, so a control Photoshop does not have does not belong in that array even when the app renders it prominently; it is recorded in `appNotes` instead, where an app-side addition belongs.

**The tool substitutes colour and preserves luminance, which is the whole of its contract and was the thing the previous version got most wrong.** `replaceColorPreservingLuminance` (`colorReplacement.ts:182`) applies a single luminance **offset**, `out = replacement + (L − L_replacement)`, so each replaced pixel keeps its own shading and only its hue and chroma move. The alternative the function's own doc comment rules out is a scale, `replacement × (L / L_replacement)`, which is exact only while the result is in gamut: above the boundary it overruns 255 on its peak channel and a hue-preserving downscale lands every brighter pixel on the same colour, so the region flattens into one solid — the exact failure the function exists to prevent. There is no spray and no forced black anywhere in this tool.

**The colour is the store's foreground, read live, and the bar writes no colour at all.** `ColorReplacementToolArea.tsx:141` builds the commit args as `{ ...args, color: store.color, points }` at pointer-up, for the reason the tool area states: the user can change the foreground while this tool stays selected, and a value captured when the bar mounted would replace with the colour that was current at the time. The options bar therefore renders the foreground as a **readout** (`ColorReplacement.tsx:179`) beside the sampled colour, and patches Tolerance, Smoothness, Size, Opacity and Limits but never a colour. The old note's claim that "only two components in the codebase ever write a paint tool's `args.color` from the store's foreground" was true when written and is now a misleading reason to distrust the result: this tool is a third such component, and it is the one that matters.

**It edits the active layer in place and never creates one.** `updateLayerByTool` (`ColorReplacementToolArea.tsx:166`), the same distinction `EraserToolArea` draws, and for the same reason: a new transparent layer holding a recolouring composites as nothing. A drag that matched nothing writes no history at all — the plan is computed on the same pixels, with the same predicate and the same settings the processor will use, and a null plan ends the gesture silently (`ColorReplacementToolArea.tsx:163-166`), which is the rule `FillToolArea` already follows for a fill that matched nothing.

**There is no live preview, and that is a deliberate, documented divergence.** Photoshop repaints the replaced pixels live on the overlay as the stroke moves. The app draws no overlay for the tool at all, for the reason its own tool area gives: an honest overlay needs the full-layer plan recomputed on every pointer move, which is a multi-megapixel tolerance scan plus a flood per move, and a cheaper overlay that painted the brush swath regardless of tolerance "would show colour over pixels the commit is about to leave alone, which is a worse lie than none."

**Keyboard.** The app reaches the tool only by cycling the Brush tile with `Shift+B` (`AppKeyboardShortcuts.tsx:18`). No letter of its own; see `## Unverified` for the Photoshop binding.

## Eraser Tool

Removes pixels to transparency under a dragged tip; on a Background layer, or one with transparency locked, to the background color instead.

**Adobe's own description of the three modes** is unusually clean: "Brush: Erases pixels with adjustable softness and opacity settings. Pencil: Erases pixels with hard edges. Block: Erases pixels in a fixed-size square shape without opacity control." And: "In Brush and Pencil modes, adjust Size, Hardness, Opacity, and Flow from the Options bar."

**Erase to History turns the same drag into a restore**, and `Alt`-dragging turns it on temporarily — Adobe: "Hold Alt (Windows) or Option (macOS) while dragging to temporarily switch to Erase to History mode."

**Bar order, and the one part of it that is inference.** Adobe's own Eraser page enumerates its bar as the `Mode` dropdown and then "In Brush and Pencil modes, adjust Size, Hardness, Opacity, and Flow from the Options bar" — so Mode precedes those four, which the table follows. **It does not position the Brush Preset Picker at all**, and no page opened in this pass places it on the Eraser's bar, so its slot at index 0 is this family's uniform placement rather than a sourced fact: the Brush, the Pencil, the Clone Stamp and the Color Replacement all put the picker leftmost (for the last two, from Adobe's "next to the tool at the upper-left" and the CS4 manual's step order), and Adobe's colour-management page puts a Brush Picker on the Eraser's own bar — "the Clone Stamp tool, the Eraser tool, the Dodge tool and more … they all have a Brush Picker in their Options bar" — without saying where. It is recorded in `## Unverified`. **What makes the Eraser a genuine exception rather than a copy of the Brush is that it keeps top-bar Size and Hardness anyway**, which is why a reader had no way to tell the two rules apart from the old file.

**`Flow` is the finding on this tool.** The bar renders Photoshop's real `Flow` label over a 1–100 range and a hardcoded 100% value — and the `<input>` carries `disabled`, so nothing is ever written. There is no `flow` field on `EraserToolArgs` at all, which means the control is not merely unwired: it has no destination. The reason is structural, and the component says so: Flow is a per-dab alpha ramp, and this renderer issues one `beginPath` / `moveTo` / `stroke()` per stroke, which has no falloff and therefore no place to apply a per-dab rate. `Opacity` is the honest substitute, and it is what the app's Opacity actually is.

**The 1–100 range is the app's, not Adobe's, and the previous version of this note called it Photoshop's.** The component's own comment claims "Photoshop's 1-100", and the bar does render `min="1"` for `Flow` while `Opacity` renders `min="0"` (`Eraser.tsx:106` against `:87`) — so the two controls on the same bar disagree about whether 0 is reachable. **Adobe's current page states the range and the default and says nothing about the minimum**; nothing in this pass found a page that does. The `Flow` detail in `paint.ts` now says exactly that and asserts no minimum, and the bound is in `## Unverified`.

**`Mode: Brush` is the same root cause, one level up.** The option is present with Adobe's own label and `disabled`, because a soft tip needs a stamped-dab engine this renderer does not have. Offering it enabled would have made a "Brush" eraser silently render as Pencil.

**What matches.** The Eraser edits the **active** layer in place by subtracting alpha (`composite: 'destination-out'`, `eraser.ts:39`) rather than creating a layer, which is what Photoshop does and which the DrawToolArea path cannot do. One history state per stroke, committed on pointer-up (`EraserToolArea.tsx:169`). Opacity is correctly removed from the bar in Block mode rather than greyed out — Adobe removes it, and the component matches.

**Keyboard.** `E` selects the Eraser, matching Photoshop. The app also reaches it by cycling the Brush tile with `Shift+B`, which is its own choice — Photoshop keeps the Eraser in its own Tools-panel slot.

## Magic Eraser Tool

The Paint Bucket run in reverse: one click erases every pixel within Tolerance of the clicked pixel. Adobe's own current page gives the bar in five lines, and the list is exact:

> In the Options bar, enter a value between 0 and 255 for Tolerance: Lower values erase colors similar to the selected pixel. Higher values erase a broad range of colors. Select Anti-alias to smooth the edges of the erased area. Select Contiguous to erase pixels of similar color adjacent to the selected pixel. Deselect Contiguous to erase all matching pixels across the layer. Select Sample All Layers to erase pixels based on colors from all visible layers or leave it unchecked to sample only from the active layer. Enter a value to set Opacity: 100% erases pixels. Lower values partially erase pixels, creating transparency.

**The control is `Anti-alias` and the previous version of this file had it as `Anti-aliased`, which is the older spelling.** The same tool's older Adobe page — `/using/tool-techniques/magic-eraser-tool.html`, last updated 2024-06-18 — says "Select Anti-aliased to smooth the edges", and the CS3 manual mirror says "Select Anti-aliased to smooth the edges of the area you erase." The **current** page, `/desktop/repair-retouch/clean-restore-images/change-similar-pixels-with-the-magic-eraser-tool.html`, last updated 2025-11-04, says "Select Anti-alias to smooth the edges of the erased area." Both URLs stay in `sources`, because the point is the version boundary and not the wording: the name moved. The Paint Bucket's row moved the same way — its 2024-05-08 page lists "Mode, Opacity, Tolerance, Anti-alias, Contiguous, and All Layers" and its current 2026-02-23 page says "Anti-alias: Create smoother edges on your filled areas", where the CS3 manual and the underwaterphotography mirror both read `Anti-aliased`. **The app renders the legacy spelling** in both bars, which is an app fact and now lives in the two `appNotes` rather than being a reason to keep Adobe's name wrong.

**Note the naming difference from the bucket, which is real and not a typo.** The bucket's checkbox is `All Layers` and the Magic Eraser's is `Sample All Layers`. They are the same idea — read the color data from the merged visible layers rather than the active one — and the two Adobe pages use the two different words.

**The cost of the tool is that there is no preview.** It works like the Magic Wand without the selection, so the region it is about to remove is visible only in the History panel afterwards. glensmith.co.uk makes the comparison plain: "with the Magic Wand you can see if any part of your subject will be selected and you can adjust this before deleting pixels. The Magic Eraser does not allow you to see this."

**Default Tolerance is 32, and 32 is third-party.** Adobe's current page gives the range and the direction and stops: "In the Options bar, enter a value between 0 and 255 for Tolerance: Lower values erase colors similar to the selected pixel. Higher values erase a broad range of colors." The 32 comes from two independent third parties — glensmith.co.uk ("The default setting is 32px and this works well for many scenarios") and an Adobe community answer from an Adobe Community Expert ("The default 32 is way to high for the background of your image—that's why it's eating away at the interior light colors"). The bar's scale of 0–255 is Adobe's, from the CS3 manual. `paint.ts` now attributes the default inline in the same form the Color Replacement tool's Tolerance row already used, rather than printing an unsourced `Default 32`.

| Tolerance         | How far a pixel's color may differ and still be erased                               | 0–255 per Adobe; 32 commonly cited | `present` | Correct scale, correct default, hard threshold                    |

**`Anti-alias` is `inert` and the reason is arithmetic.** It renders the app's own legacy `Anti-aliased` label and Photoshop's real checked state, and the DOM input is `disabled`. The flood fill's test is a hard threshold (`isPixelWithinTolerance`, `floodFill.ts:76`) producing a `Uint8Array` mask of 0s and 1s, so there is no coverage value to smooth — anti-aliasing needs a fractional boundary, and this tool never computes one.

**Tolerance is on the right scale, which is a real and easy thing to get wrong.** The Magic Wand uses 0–100 against a 4-channel distance that includes alpha; the bucket, the Magic Eraser and the Background Eraser use 0–255 against a 3-channel RGB distance, where a pixel differing only in alpha is _inside_ the region. The app gets this right, and the two scales cannot drift into each other because both bars render against the same shared constant.

**Version boundary — the Background layer rule is the Eraser's, not the Magic Eraser's, and the two tools disagree.** This is the one place where a current Photoshop claim and a pre-CS phrasing conflict outright, and the resolution is that they are describing **different tools**.

- **Eraser.** Adobe's current page (last updated 2026-02-23): "If erasing on the Background layer or a locked transparent layer, the erased area is filled with the background color." Adobe's CS3 manual, same rule: "If you're working in the background or in a layer with transparency locked, the pixels change to the background color; otherwise, the pixels are erased to transparency."
- **Magic Eraser.** Adobe's current page (last updated 2026-02-23): "If you select the background layer, Adobe Photoshop automatically converts it into a regular layer before erasing similar pixels" — so a Background layer is **not** a background-colour case; it is converted and then cleared to transparency. Adobe's CS3 manual says the same in one sentence: "If you're working in a layer with locked transparency, the pixels change to the background color. If you click in the background, it is converted to a layer and all similar pixels change to transparent."

An earlier version of this file asserted both behaviours in one sentence for the Magic Eraser, which is self-contradictory and was refuted by the very page it cited. The confusable pre-CS phrasing — "if you're working in the background, or in a layer with locked transparency, the pixels change to the background color" — is the **Eraser** rule, and it is the wording that survives to the present day on Adobe's Eraser page. Third-party prose blurs the pair further: dummies.com's Elements 9 walkthrough states the Magic Eraser's rule correctly ("Unless you're working on a layer with the transparency locked, the pixels are erased to transparency. If you're working on an image with just a background, Elements converts the background into a layer") while its adjacent sentence about the plain Eraser keeps the older conflation. The rule for this file is therefore: **locked transparency → background colour, on either tool; Background layer → background colour for the Eraser, auto-converted to a normal layer and cleared to transparency for the Magic Eraser.**

The app refuses the Bedrock layer outright and treats every other layer identically, so it diverges from Photoshop under either rule.

**Keyboard.** Photoshop's own help page prints "(E)" for this tool, which contradicts the usual convention that `E` is the Eraser group; no source was found binding the Magic Eraser to a letter of its own. The app's `G`/`Shift+G` fill group contains the Paint Bucket and the Gradient only, so the Magic Eraser has no shortcut at all.

## Paint Bucket Tool

Fills the whole connected region of similar color around the click in a single click. Shares a Tools-panel slot with the Gradient tool; Adobe's page says so and the `G` / `Shift+G` cycle follows from it.

**Adobe's bar, in their order**: Fill (Foreground or Pattern), then under Blending the Mode, then Opacity, then Tolerance, then Anti-alias, Contiguous and All Layers. Adobe's Tolerance note is the useful one: "Lower values (10-20) fill similar colors, while higher values (100 or more) fill a broader range," and "Lower opacity values create more transparent fills." Adobe gives the bucket's Tolerance range and gives no default, so the widely-cited 32 is third-party here exactly as it is on the Magic Eraser, and `paint.ts` attributes it inline.

**`Mode` here is the 27 layer blend modes plus `Clear`, not plus `Dissolve`, `Behind` and `Clear`.** Dissolve is one of the 27 — Adobe's own `BlendMode` enum has `DISSOLVE` in it — and `Behind` is the Brush's alone, working "only in layers with Lock Transparency deselected". `Clear` is the one this tool gets, alongside the shape tools, the Brush, the Pencil, the Fill command and the Stroke command. The old wording double-counted Dissolve on all three tools that carried it and gave the bucket a `Behind` it does not have; the Brush, the Pencil, the bucket and the Clone Stamp rows have all been corrected against Adobe's blend-mode descriptions page and the UXP constants reference.

| Photoshop control | What it does                                                     | Default / range                    | App       | Why                                                                                                      |
| ----------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- | --------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Fill              | Foreground, or Pattern (which enables a second dropdown)         | Foreground                         | `partial` | `Foreground` enabled and live; `Pattern` `disabled` at `PaintBucket.tsx:51` — no pattern registry exists |
| Mode              | Blend mode for the fill                                          | Normal                             | `missing` | `FillToolArgs` declares no blend mode, and the fill writes into the _active_ layer's pixels              |
| Opacity           | Lower values create more transparent fills                       | 100; 0–100                         | `present` | Real straight-alpha source-over                                                                          |
| Tolerance         | Range of colors filled                                           | 0–255 per Adobe; 32 commonly cited | `present` | Shared with the Magic Eraser against one constant                                                        |
| Anti-alias        | Smooths the filled area's edges                                  | on                                 | `inert`   | Same hard-threshold mask as the Magic Eraser                                                             |
| Contiguous        | On: only pixels contiguous to the click. Off: all similar pixels | on                                 | `present` | Shared flood-fill code                                                                                   |
| All Layers        | Fill from the merged color data of all visible layers            | off                                | `missing` | Reads only the active layer                                                                              |

**`Mode` is `missing` for a structural reason that is worth more than the fact itself.** The Paint Bucket is a `commit: 'raster'` tool that edits the **active** layer's pixels in place, so a blend mode here would be a per-pixel composite against a destination the processor does not address — unlike the Brush, where the stroke creates its own layer and a layer blend mode is exactly the right seam. Adding it needs both an args field and a different composite primitive.

**`Pattern` is `partial` rather than `missing` because half of it works.** The `Fill` control itself is real: the readout shows the store's foreground color live, so a user can see what the click will paint, and `Foreground` is the one fill the tool performs. The `Pattern` option is present with its real label and `disabled`.

**The app renders the legacy `Anti-aliased` label on this bar, and the table says `Anti-alias`.** The checkbox in the shared fill bar is hardcoded to the older spelling, which Adobe's current Paint Bucket page has dropped. The control is inert either way, so nothing about the app's behaviour changes; the divergence that matters is that a screenshot of this bar does not read the way Adobe's current page describes it, and that belongs in `appNotes` rather than in a control name.

**The one place this implementation is better than the obvious version.** The fill is a real source-over (`blendPixel`, `fill.ts:151`), not a lerp of straight RGB against straight RGB. The difference is visible at partial opacity over a transparent region, where a lerp writes half the fill color mixed with half of nothing — half-black — and correct compositing writes half coverage of clean red. Adobe's own sentence, "Lower opacity values create more transparent fills," only holds if the fill color survives the blend.

**The commit is the click, with no drag.** `FillToolArea` handles `pointerdown` and nothing else, by design: Photoshop commits the fill on the click, so a drag-to-adjust gesture would add a second history state the product does not have. A fill that matches nothing writes no history at all — the mask is computed before the commit, and a null result ends the gesture silently.

**Keyboard.** `G` selects it, `Shift+G` cycles back from the Gradient. Matches Photoshop.

## Clone Stamp Tool

Copies pixels from one part of an image and paints them into another. Adobe's gesture, verbatim: "Press the Alt key (Windows) or the Option key (macOS) and click the area of the image that you want to copy. This sets a starting point from which the tool will begin copying. Release the Alt or Option key and drag over the new area where you want to place your copied content."

**Full bar, left to right** — `[Tool presets] | [Brush presets] | Mode ▾ | Opacity :100% | Flow :100% | ⊙Pressure-for-opacity | ☑Aligned | Sample ▾ | ☐Ignore Adjustment Layers | ⊙Pressure-for-size | [Clone Source / Brush panel toggle]`.

**The `Tool Preset Picker` row was missing from the table while the section's own bar list already opened with `[Tool presets]`, and the `Brush Preset Picker` detail already quoted Adobe's sentence about it.** That is the shape of the omission: the control was described inside a neighbouring row and drawn in the bar list, and had no row of its own — so `types.ts`'s "every control Photoshop places in this tool's options bar, in bar order" was not met, and the record's own `appNotes` count of "ten controls" was counting a bar one control shorter than Adobe's. It is now the first row, `missing` because nothing in the app renders it, and the count is eleven.

`Flow` and the airbrush semantics are the Brush tool's, already researched there, so they are not re-derived. `Sample` and `Ignore Adjustment Layers` are Adobe's CS4 manual verbatim, and the manual names the position of the second control, which is the part most third-party pages get wrong:

> Sample — Samples data from the layers you specify. To sample from the active layer and visible layers below it, choose Current And Below. To sample only from the active layer, choose Current Layer. To sample from all visible layers, choose All Layers. To sample from all visible layers **except adjustment layers**, choose All Layers and click the **Ignore Adjustment Layers icon to the right of the Sample pop-up menu**.

The button was added in CS3 as an unlabeled toggle, per properproof's write-up: "Adobe Photoshop CS3 includes a small improvement… a new, unlabeled button that lets you ignore all adjustment layers while cloning," motivated by a concrete failure — "If we start using our clone tool with the color adjustment layer visible, and our clone tool set to 'Sample All Layers' we end up cloning with a weird green hue." Four independent sources place it immediately right of the Sample menu: two mirrors of the CS4 manual, jkost, and underwaterphotoshop.

**Aligned is two different rules, and this is where the app is wrong.** Adobe's own clone-stamp page states the pair, and it is the stroke-to-stroke rule verbatim: "Select **Aligned to paint the most recent sampling point. Deselect Aligned to restart sampling from the original point each time.**" That sentence alone establishes the anchor changes on every stroke when Aligned is on and does not when it is off, which is the whole of the rule the app diverges from, and it is the citation this should rest on.

The **mechanism** behind "the most recent sampling point" is stated more explicitly only by uidaho's tool-bar page — a 1990s course reference, not Adobe, and now returning 403 to a direct fetch, so it is retained as corroboration and labelled as what it is rather than leading the paragraph:

> If you have Aligned checked, your clones will be applied as if you were working with **a copy of the image that has been displaced the distance of your first stroke after Alt-clicking**. All subsequent strokes (prior to another Alt-click) will fill in the copied image relative to that first stroke. When you do not have Aligned checked, all (separate) strokes after an Alt-click will apply **from the single spot that you Alt-clicked**. Each time you end one stroke and make a new one, the new one will use the same starting spot.

So the anchor is established **once, at the first stroke after the Alt-click, and persists across strokes** until another Alt-click replaces it. Aligned and unaligned are the same subtraction against two different anchors: a stored one versus the fixed Alt-clicked point. The same page states the unaligned consequence for the reader directly: "Thus you can repeatedly apply the same image data (the Alt-click location) to different spots."

**The app implements neither arm.** `cloneOffset` at `src/tools/cloneStamp.ts:89` takes the **current gesture's** path and anchors to `points[0]` when aligned, `points[points.length - 1]` when not. There is no field anywhere on `CloneStampToolArgs` for a stored offset. Consequences:

- _Aligned_: the offset is constant within one stroke — which matches — and re-anchors to each new stroke's own start, which is precisely the drift the mode exists to remove. The control is wired and reads as its own value; it is `partial`, not `present`.
- _Unaligned_: the read point slides backwards by the drag's displacement as the gesture progresses, rather than staying at the Alt-clicked spot with only the destination moving.

Both arms are one subtraction, so this is a **missing anchor, not a wrong formula** — the fix is a stored offset on the args, not new pixel code. This is the single most consequential finding on this tool.

**The source-not-output guarantee is real, and it holds for a structural reason worth naming twice.** Every read in `cloneDab` indexes an immutable copy of the raster and every write indexes the buffer, so no dab can sample a texel a previous dab laid down. The copy is taken once per _processor invocation_, and the guarantee upgrades to once per _stroke_ for a second reason: the live preview re-runs the processor on every pointer move but always against the store's own pre-stroke raster, never the previous frame's preview — `src/contexts/useLayer.ts:557` reads `layers[id].tmpCanvas ?? layers[id].bitmap` from the store layer, while the accumulated preview lives in `previewLayersRef` and is never the input. The commit then re-runs the processor once over the whole path before publishing. So neither the live preview nor the committed stroke can compound itself.

**The Photoshop side of that is not a documented guarantee, and the honest record says so.** See `## Unverified`: the widely repeated "Photoshop samples the source once at stroke start" rule is folklore with no Adobe source. What _is_ sourced is the artifact and its documented remedy — the classic failure being that the source cursor reaching the area already painted makes the removed thing reappear, with the advice to "clone-stamp a little bit at a time… stamp a little, release, stamp a little, release" (graphicdesign.stackexchange). That the remedy is needed is itself the evidence that a per-stroke snapshot is not something Photoshop can be relied on to provide, which is what makes the app's structural version worth stating as a divergence in its favour.

**Layer edge cases.** Adobe states one directly: "Make sure you are not working on an adjustment layer. The Clone Stamp tool does not work on adjustment layers." The app's gate (`CloneStampToolArea.tsx:210`) refuses the Bedrock layer and requires a source, but it **does not check the layer's lock flag** — and `psd-utils.ts:268` round-trips that lock as `protected: { composite, position }`. So a locked layer will still accept a cloned stroke, and a file saved from that state is protected in a reader and freely overwritable in the editor. That is the opposite failure direction from the Eraser's, which at least honours the Bedrock refusal.

**The scope of the lock gap is narrow, and an earlier version of this note overstated it.** It said "a layer the UI presents as locked", which is a claim about a screen the app does not have. `layer.locked` is declared in `layer-types.ts:59` and is set true in exactly one place: the PSD import walk (`psd-utils.ts:128` folds a child's own lock into its parent's, and `psd-utils.ts:160` writes the result onto the layer). Every other construction path writes `false` — a new layer at `layer-utils.ts:188`, the background layer at `psd-utils.ts:192`, a replayed document at `history-adapter.ts:767`. There is no lock control in any panel and no lock icon anywhere in the UI, so a user cannot set one and cannot see one. The finding is therefore real and worth keeping, but it is an imported-file finding rather than a live-UI one, and both `paint.ts` and this note now say that instead of implying a control that does not exist.

`Ignore Adjustment Layers` has nothing to ignore here even in principle: a layer source in this app is `blank`, `image`, `tool` or an `affine-transform` operation, and Levels, Color, Transform and Blur are destructive operations on a raster rather than layers sitting above a clone target.

**The Clone Source panel** holds up to five sampling sources, "these can be from the same document or from multiple documents" (photoshopcafe), with per-source flip, rotate, scale and opacity, a Show Overlay checkbox, and a Clipped option that limits the overlay to the brush size. Since CS4 the sampled source is displayed live under the brush, which hoffmanartdesign credits to a feature "migrated over from the Vanishing Point filter" and describes as "a colored blob that is the source of confusion for many people." None of it exists in the app; the only trace of the idea is a crosshair drawn on the overlay canvas.

**What matches.** The Alt-click sets the source at **pointer-down** and paints nothing; the decision is made at down, so a drag that began as a plain press keeps cloning even if the modifier is released mid-stroke, and a press that began with it down never starts a stroke at all. Both the source and the stroke are rebased into the active layer's frame, which is safe precisely because the processor only uses their _difference_. With no source set the gesture is **refused** — not-allowed cursor, no stroke, no history entry — rather than accepted and committed as a no-op. The copy carries the source texel's own alpha, so cloning out of a transparent region leaves the destination transparent instead of painting it opaque black. Size, Hardness and Opacity are all wired, and the tip is borrowed from the shared brush engine, so a soft Clone edge is the same soft edge.
