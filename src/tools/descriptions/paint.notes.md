# Paint family — research notes

The seven tools whose work is putting pixels on, taking them off, or moving them: `brush`, `pencil`, `color-replacement`, `eraser`, `magic-eraser`, `paint-bucket`, `clone-stamp`.

**How to read the tables.** `Status` is the app's fidelity for that one Photoshop control, checked against **both** `src/tools/settings.ts` and the tool's own component under `src/components/ToolbarsRight/` before anything was called `missing`. `present` renders and behaves; `partial` renders but diverges; `missing` renders nothing; `inert` renders with Photoshop's own label but writes nothing. `appRef` points at the **wiring**, not at the label: for a live control that is the handler that writes the args, and for an `inert` one it is the `disabled` DOM node itself, because on an inert control the disabled node _is_ the finding.

**A note on `modifiers` and `shortcuts`.** `ToolModifier` (`src/tools/descriptions/types.ts`) has no `status` and no `appRef`, so a modifier row can only carry `keys` and `action`. Whether the app honours one is therefore recorded in `appNotes` and here, never in the row. That is a shape constraint, not a choice: this pass began writing `status` onto modifier rows and had to take it out again.

**On the currency of the citations, precisely, because two earlier versions of this sentence were false claims rather than stale ones.** The first read "every line number in this file and in `paint.ts` was re-derived against this worktree from the meaning of its claim", and the coordinates refuted it — the citations it had written down, and what each one named, were these:

```text
settings.ts:136          settings.ts:154          brush.ts:23
toolbarPlaceholderConfig.ts:4     ToolbarPlaceholder.tsx:25-32
```

The first two named the Brush's and the Pencil's `alpha: 0.9`, whose real homes are the `brush` and `pencil` entries of `defaultSettings` in `src/tools/settings.ts`; the third named `PAINT_BLEND_MODES`, whose declaration is `PAINT_BLEND_MODES` itself in `src/tools/brush.ts`; and the last two named a Color Replacement placeholder bar that no longer exists. The second, softer version said the unrewritten citations "were each re-read and left alone only because they still hold" — and **this pass falsified that too**: all seven `settings.ts` citations in `paint.ts` were off, by between one and nine lines, in one direction, always early. They pointed at the closing brace of the _previous_ tool's block. Four of the `Paint.tsx` / `Eraser.tsx` citations were off as well, one of them at a blank line and one at a comment. **What is true now, and is the whole claim this file makes: every citation in `paint.ts` and in this file was re-derived by opening the target it names and reading it, and the ones with no load-bearing claim were checked the same way.** Every citation in `paint.ts` is now a handler that writes the args, a `disabled` node, or the `useKeyDownShortcut` call that registers the key it names — no exceptions, checked by dumping all 82 of them with the text each stood beside. No bar in this family is a `PlaceholderTool` any more, so the `inert` caveat about chip labels that do not carry their own control's name applies to no row in this file.

**Version.** Photoshop 2024–2026 throughout. Two version boundaries are load-bearing and are named where they occur: `Smoothing` and `Symmetry` are 2017/18-era additions, and the option-bar `Hardness` control is gone from the bars of the tools that select their tip through the Brush Preset picker — the Brush, the Pencil and the Clone Stamp. It is **not** gone everywhere: Adobe's current Eraser page still says "In Brush and Pencil modes, adjust Size, Hardness, Opacity, and Flow from the Options bar", so the Eraser keeps both on its bar, which is why the Brush and Pencil rows read as an inconsistency until that sentence is in the same file as them.

**Retrieval boundary.** Adobe's `helpx.adobe.com` pages were read through the live search crawler this pass, which is the only route that returns their text; a direct fetch of one Adobe-hosted mirror timed out and is not evidence of anything either way. Two Adobe manuals are cited through mirrors of Adobe's own text (`manualsdir.com`, `adobe.manymanuals.com`, and a Wacom-hosted PDF of the CC 2018 help), and one — the blend-mode descriptions page — was confirmed through a photographic club's PDF of that page, which reproduces Adobe's current wording including the "– Brush blend mode" suffixes. Each is labelled as a mirror where it is load-bearing, because the mirror is not Adobe but the words are Adobe's.

## Brush Tool

Photoshop's general painting tool. Click and drag to lay down a stroke of the foreground color on the active layer.

**Load-bearing distinction — Opacity is not Flow. Adobe now states it, and this file previously said it did not.** Opacity is a hard ceiling for one stroke: the stroke cannot exceed the set level however many times the pointer crosses the same pixels, and only a new stroke re-applies it. Flow is the per-dab build-up _inside_ that ceiling. **An earlier version of this paragraph opened "No Adobe page states an accumulation formula for either setting" and that sentence is now falsified.** `painting-tools.html` gives the ceiling — "As you paint over an area, the opacity does not exceed the set level no matter how many times you move the pointer over the area, until you release the mouse button. If you stroke over the area again, you apply additional color, equivalent to the set opacity" — and then a worked example of the build-up, not just its existence: "if you set the opacity to 33% and the flow to 33%, each time you move over an area, its color moves 33% toward the brush color. The total will not exceed 33% opacity unless you release the mouse button and stroke over the area again." That is the accumulate-under-a-clamp model, from Adobe, with numbers: each pass moves a fixed fraction of the remaining gap and the sequence converges to the Opacity. **What is still not Adobe's is the closed form.** The single expression that names the ceiling directly is the "over" composition of the two rates, `ps = flow + op − flow·op`, which is why it appears so often; Adobe's 33/33 example states the iteration, not that expression, and nothing in `paint.ts` asserts the expression.

Krita's manual documents the same ceiling semantics from the other side, having changed flow and opacity from "add up to one another" to multiplying in 4.2 "so in 4.2 this finally got corrected to the flow and opacity multiplying, resulting in much more subtle strokes" — an independent application that treats Opacity as a maximum and Flow as a per-dab rate, which is the pair `paint.ts` asserts. Adobe's own example is consistent with it: 33% flow at 33% opacity, iterated, lands on 33% and stops.

This is the single most commonly mis-implemented pair in a paint engine, and it is why `Flow` is `missing` for the app's Brush rather than `partial`.

**The pair is sourced, and the third-party pages below are corroboration rather than the citation.** Two Adobe pages now carry the wording and both are in `sources`. What follows is retained because it states the ceiling in one sentence each and is worth keeping readable, and because it is the corpus a reviewer will reach for — but each is a third party and neither is what the claim now rests on. asktimgrey.com's _Flow versus Opacity_ states the cap and the clamp in one sentence — "If you paint back and forth without releasing the mouse, you'll build up the effect in all areas that you paint over more than once, but that build up will **never exceed 50% strength based on the Opacity setting**" — and, separately, that at Opacity 50 with Flow 100 you "keep the mouse button held down and paint back and forth over the same area, you'll still only be painting with 50% gray, without having the painting effect building up based on passing over the same area multiple times." It also states the re-application half, which the data file's "a new stroke re-applies it" rests on: "Even with the Opacity set to a reduced value, however, if you release the mouse and then paint over the same area again, the effect will build up." photoshopcafe.com's _Should you use Flow or Opacity_ says the same in the demonstration: "No matter how hard I press, I'm going to get 50 percent… It's not going to go more than 50 percent," and then, lifting the pen, "I can do another 50 percent."

**The Bar Has No Top-Bar Size, and that is a version fact the file previously got backwards for two of the three tools it applied it to.** Current Photoshop's Brush bar carries no top-bar `Size` and no top-bar `Hardness`; both are set inside the Brush Preset picker popout. Adobe's own words, from `painting-tools.html`: "You can quickly choose presets from the Brush Preset picker in the options bar, which lets you temporarily modify the size and hardness of a brush preset," and the Brush-presets page adds that such a change is "temporary if you modify a preset brush, such as changing its size or hardness." The retouch page states the same rule for that family: "Set the brush's Size, Hardness, and Spacing from the brush options dropdown menu in the Options bar." **The Clone Stamp row already said this and the Brush and Pencil rows did not, which is the inconsistency that was worth fixing** — not because the Eraser contradicts it, but because the Eraser legitimately keeps both on its bar, so a reader had no way to tell which rule applied where. The 1–5000 figure is unchanged and now lives in the `Brush Preset Picker` detail, where Adobe puts it.

**Bar order, and this pass got it wrong once and had to re-derive it.** The previous version said "Bar order is Tool Preset Picker, then Brush Preset Picker, then the rest" — a sentence that named the first two controls and no others, so it neither was nor claimed to be a full order. **The full order, from three independent current walks of the bar, is:**

> `[Tool presets] | [Brush presets] | [Brush Settings panel toggle] | Mode ▾ | Opacity :100% | ⊙Pressure-for-opacity | Flow :100% | [airbrush] | Smoothing :10% ⚙ | Brush Angle | ⊙Pressure-for-size | [symmetry]`

**The Brush Settings panel toggle is third, immediately right of the two pickers and before Mode** — Practical Photoshop 2022's numbered callouts for the Brush put it at (3) with the pickers at (1) and (2) and Painting Modes at (4), and glensmith's Brush page walks the bar in the same left-to-right order with "Brushes Panel" third. The previous version had it last, at the far right of the bar, which is the one placement both of those sources exclude.

**The Brush Angle control is real and was missing from the table entirely.** Three current sources name it on the Brush's bar and all three place it after Smoothing and before Symmetry: Practical Photoshop 2022 ("10 | Brush Angle", with a worked exercise "6.6: Use the Brush Angle Setting"); Wacom's guide to Photoshop brushes, which distinguishes it from the popout widget explicitly — "With this you can set an exact brush angle by entering a number in degrees, or clicking the icon and dragging L or R. **This is much more precise than the arrow in the Brush Preset Window**"; and glensmith's Brush page, which reuses one screenshot of the widget across the Brush, the Pencil, the Eraser, the Clone Stamp and the Color Replacement bars. It is `missing` in the app on all five. **The one part of the order that is inference rather than observation is where the airbrush toggle and the two pressure toggles sit relative to each other.** Practical Photoshop's numbered list omits the pressure toggles entirely; glensmith puts Pressure Sensitive Opacity immediately after Opacity and Pressure Sensitive Size after Brush Angle. The order recorded above follows glensmith, and it is the only sequence here with one witness per position.

The Tool Preset Picker is first because Adobe places it by position and not by name: "You can also select the Tool Presets dropdown menu next to the tool at the upper-left of the Options bar." The Brush Preset Picker is the leftmost of the bar's own controls once that one is set aside, which is how the `Size` detail came to be folded into it.

**Mode is a layer property, not a stroke property.** The app seeds the created layer's `blendMode` from the tool args. That is a defensible simplification for a Normal-mode brush and a visible divergence for the other fifteen.

**The blend-mode list was wrong in two ways, and both are the kind of error a count hides.** The old text said the Brush offers "the 27 layer blend modes plus Dissolve, Behind and Clear", which counts Dissolve twice — **Dissolve is one of the 27**, as Adobe's own `BlendMode` enum shows (`DISSOLVE` in the list at the UXP constants reference, alongside `PASSTHROUGH` and the 25 others). And the availability is not uniform across the family: **Behind is the Brush's alone**, documented as working "only in layers with Lock Transparency deselected" and as a Brush blend mode, while **Clear goes to "the Shape tools (when fill region is selected), Paint Bucket tool, Brush tool, Pencil tool, Fill command, and Stroke command"**. So the Brush's list is the 27 plus two, the Pencil's and the Paint Bucket's are the 27 plus `Clear`, and the Clone Stamp's is the 27 with neither. The Brush's total is 29, not "thirty-plus" — the old `appNotes` said "thirty-plus" and that has been corrected to 29.

**The Tool Preset Picker is a current control, and a removal claim here was wrong.** Photoshop's bar opens with a **Tool Preset Picker** at the far left, beside the tool icon, whose menu carries `New Tool Preset`, `Rename Tool Preset`, `Delete Tool Preset`, `Reset Tool` and `Reset All Tools`. An earlier pass recorded this control as gone, on the strength of one secondary source: clippingpathca.com (2025) says Tool Presets "had their own dropdown menu at the far left of the Options bar" in "Photoshop CS6 or early CC versions" and that they are "now found inside the Brush Settings or Presets interface." **That is refuted by Adobe's own current page**, last updated 2026-02-23, which states: "You can also select the Tool Presets dropdown menu next to the tool at the upper-left of the Options bar," with a Create New Tool Preset icon on it. Julieanne Kost's Adobe blog independently gives the same two access routes and the right-click `Reset Tool` / `Reset All Tools` items. A blog post is not a sufficient authority for an absence, and here it was not even right.

So the row is `missing` because the **app** does not render the control, not because Photoshop has dropped it — and the Brush, Pencil and Clone Stamp rows all say so explicitly, with Adobe's sentence quoted. The app's `Brush Preset` select — three synthetic settings snapshots plus a `Custom` row, written by `handlePresetChange` in `Paint.tsx` — is the app's own addition. That is unaffected: it is not standing in for a control Photoshop renders, because it applies options-bar state in a position Photoshop's own picker also occupies while offering no picker of its own.

**There is no `Size` row, and that is a finding rather than an omission.** Photoshop's own `Size` is a real control and its 1–5000 range is correct; current Photoshop just does not put it on this bar. The app does, which is a divergence from Photoshop's bar and not from Photoshop's control, so it is recorded in the `Brush Preset Picker` detail (where Adobe puts it) and in `appNotes` (where the app's own addition belongs), and it is deliberately absent from this table — `ToolDescription` in `src/tools/descriptions/types.ts` reserves `options[]` for controls Photoshop places in the bar. The app's `Size` slider is rendered by `PaintToolbar` in `Paint.tsx` and written by that component's `handleSizeChange`, capping at `PAINT_SIZE_MAX` in `src/tools/brush.ts`.

**`Smoothing` is not the only control these three bars share, and the old note here said it was.** The Brush `Smoothing` detail claimed to be "the only control on the bar the Pencil and the Eraser share with the Brush", which is false on two counts: Opacity and Symmetry are on all three bars too. Adobe's symmetry page scopes the butterfly button to "the Paint Brush, Pencil, and Eraser tools" and the Eraser page keeps Opacity on the bar (withdrawing it only in Block mode), so the honest statement is that three controls carry across, and that is what the detail now says.

**Smoothing: same name, different algorithm.** The app pulls every interior sample toward the midpoint of its neighbours by `smoothing/100` (`smoothStrokePoints()` in `src/tools/brush.ts`, consumed by `drawLine()` in `src/util/draw.ts`), which is a point-space filter. Photoshop's is a trailing anchor over the **raw** input stream at input rate, which is what makes Stroke Catch-up, Catch-up on Stroke End and Pulled String Mode expressible and what couples smoothing to zoom. The app's version is recognizably the same feature and not the same mechanism — hence `partial`, not `missing`.

**What matches.** One history state per stroke: the commit is the pointer-up (`handlePointerUp` in `src/components/Main/DrawToolArea.tsx`). Opacity as a per-stroke ceiling. Smoothing at Photoshop's own 10% default (`PAINT_SMOOTHING_DEFAULT` in `src/tools/brush.ts`).

**What does not match, and is easy to miss: the app's default Opacity is 90, not 100.** The `brush` entry of `defaultSettings` in `src/tools/settings.ts` seeds the brush's `alpha: 0.9`, and its `pencil` entry does the same for the pencil, so a fresh document opens with the bar reading 90% where Photoshop's reads 100%. The control is still `present` and not `partial`: a wrong default is a default, not a divergence in what the control does, and the per-stroke-ceiling behaviour itself is exactly right. `ToolControl` — its `detail` field, in `src/tools/descriptions/types.ts` — requires the difference to be written down rather than left for a reader to discover, so the `Opacity` rows in `paint.ts` carry it in `detail` and both `appNotes` repeat it. The Brush's own three synthetic presets carry their own `alpha` values and overwrite the seed on selection, so 90% is what a user sees on open, not an invariant.

**The largest divergence is not on the bar.** A brush stroke is committed by `createLayerFromTool` — it creates a **new layer** rather than painting the active one. Photoshop's Brush paints into the active layer. Nothing in the options table shows this.

**Modifiers and shortcuts, and the previous version of this paragraph was wrong about the app in both directions.** It read "`B` is the only one this app binds … the global table registers no bracket key, no digit key, and no Alt or Shift override for any paint tool." **The first half is now false.** `AppKeyboardShortcuts.tsx` carries two per-tool lookup tables that the previous pass read past:

- `tipSizeField` (in `src/components/Common/AppKeyboardShortcuts.tsx`) maps `brush`, `pencil`, `eraser`, `color-replacement`, `smudge`, `dodge`, `burn` and `clone-stamp` to each one's size `<input>` id, and that file's `useKeyDownShortcut` registrations for `[` and `]` are enabled against it.
- `opacityField` (the same file) maps `brush`, `pencil`, `eraser`, `color-replacement` and `clone-stamp` to each opacity `<input>` id, and the `useKeyDownShortcut` registrations for `0` and `1` are enabled against it.

So `[`, `]`, `0` and `1` are bound for the Brush, the Pencil, the Color Replacement Tool, the Eraser and the Clone Stamp, and the four rows in `paint.ts` describe **both** Photoshop's bindings and this app's. The Brush, Pencil and Eraser `appNotes` now say so, and the Eraser and Clone Stamp `shortcuts` arrays gained the four rows they were missing.

**The second half still holds, and it is about the MODIFIERS.** There is no Alt or Shift pointer-time binding for any paint tool in that file: every `shiftKey` in it belongs to a tool-cycle group, of which `brushGroup` is the one that also reaches the pencil, the color replacement and the eraser. Outside it, `altKey` is read in a pointer handler by six tools — the shape (`handlePointerDown` in `src/components/Main/ShapeToolArea.tsx`), the rectangular marquee (`sampleFreshDragModifiers()` in `src/hooks/useRect.ts`), the magnetic lasso (`startDraft()` in `src/components/Main/FeatherMagneticLassoToolArea.tsx`), the elliptical selection (`handlePointerMove` in `src/components/Main/EllipticalSelectionToolArea.tsx`), the eyedropper (`handlePointerDown` in `src/components/Main/EyedropperToolArea.tsx`) and the clone stamp (`handlePointerDown` in `src/components/Main/CloneStampToolArea.tsx`) — and `DrawToolArea.tsx`, which is the Brush's and the Pencil's whole gesture surface, reads no modifier at all. So the `Alt` and `Shift` modifier rows describe Photoshop only. **The Eraser is the interesting case and it was missed in both directions**: `handlePointerDown` in `src/components/Main/EraserToolArea.tsx` reads `event.shiftKey` and anchors a stroke on a plain click to draw from that anchor on a Shift-click, which is Adobe's two-click straight-line gesture — so the Eraser honours its `Shift` row and does not honour its `Alt` row. `ToolModifier` carries no `status` field, so that fact lives here and in the Eraser's `appNotes` rather than on the row.

Note that the clone stamp's `Alt` row is the opposite case again and is genuinely implemented, which is what makes the brush and pencil rows worth stating rather than leaving tacit.

**Airbrush reverses the digit binding, and Adobe's own document says so.** The `Airbrush` row claims that with the toggle on the number keys drive Flow and `Shift` with them drives Opacity. That was previously recorded as unverified, and a reviewer's counter-example pointed the other way — photoshopcafe.com's Flow/Opacity page says "the shift key will enable us to do the same thing with the shortcuts, but for Flow. So Shift five will give us 50% flow." That is not a contradiction, it is the **non**-Airbrush case: that page is describing the plain tool, and Adobe's CS3 shortcuts sheet states the reversal explicitly and names the exception. Verbatim: "To change the focus from Opacity to Flow (on the options bar) add the Shift key. Note, when the Airbrush attribute is assigned to a tool, then the tapping a number changes the Flow by default (adding the Shift key changes the Opacity)." The data file's sentence is Adobe's sentence. What the reviewer's quote does establish is that the two behaviours are routinely conflated by second-hand sources, which is worth knowing; the CS3 sheet's version is recorded in `## Unverified` as the open item, not the binding itself.

**`Protect Texture` and `Protect Tones` are two different controls, and the resemblance trips readers.** `Protect Texture` is a checkbox in the **Brush Settings panel**, and it belongs to the Brush's tip model. `Protect Tones` is a checkbox on the **Dodge/Burn group's** options bar, and it is not on the Brush's bar at all. Nothing here is inconsistent: the Brush Settings panel's last entry is `Protect Texture` and no row in `paint.ts` names `Protect Tones`, because the Dodge/Burn tools are not part of this file.

## Pencil Tool

A Brush whose tip is always hard-edged. Adobe's own one-line distinction: "The Brush tool creates soft strokes of color. The Pencil tool creates hard-edged lines."

**The hard edge is not antialiasing.** Two independent sources state it: the O'Reilly _Photoshop Elements_ Missing Manual — "it offers only hard-edged brushes. In other words, you can't draw fuzzy lines with the pencil"; and dummies.com, on the partial-sketch feature — "the edges of a pencil stroke can't even be antialiased." Ask Tim Grey's sharpening is that this is what creates the jagged stair-step diagonal: a round tip at 100% hardness with no antialiasing rasterizes as a chain of square pixels. That is also why the Pencil's default tip is the 1-pixel brush (dummies.com), and why the missing control most people would name first is not a rendering bug but a tip-model one.

**Auto Erase is decided once, at mouse-down.** Adobe's exact wording, from the CS3 manual: "If the center of the cursor is over the foreground color when you begin dragging, the area is erased to the background color. If the center of the cursor is over an area that doesn't contain the foreground color when you begin dragging, the area is painted with the foreground color." Crossing the other color mid-stroke does not switch modes. This is the only genuinely Pencil-specific feature, and it is `missing` in the app for a structural reason worth stating plainly: **the app has no background color in its tool state**, so the checkbox has nothing to choose between.

**Bar order follows the Brush's, with three controls moved and two added.** The Tool Preset Picker is at the upper left, so it is index 0 and the Brush Preset Picker follows it; there is no top-bar `Size`, and the 1–5000 figure folds into the picker's detail. `Mode` is the 27 layer blend modes plus `Clear` only — `Behind` is the Brush's alone. glensmith's Pencil page walks the bar as Tool Presets, Brush Presets, Brush Settings panel, Brushes panel, Mode, Opacity, Pressure Sensitive Opacity, Smoothing, Brush Angle, Auto Erase, Pressure Sensitive Size, Symmetry — **which adds three controls the previous table did not have**: the Brush Settings panel toggle, `Always Use Pressure for Opacity`, `Always Use Pressure for Size` and `Brush Angle`. All four are `missing` in the app, and all four are asserted on one current walkthrough rather than on two, which `## Unverified` records.

**Flow really is absent from this bar, and that is now double-sourced.** The previous citation was Ask Tim Grey alone ("The Pencil tool also lacks the Flow setting on the Options bar"). glensmith's Pencil page states it structurally — "The Brush tool has the Flow and Spray Paint options, which the Pencil tool does not have. The Pencil tool has Auto Erase which the Brush tool does not have" — which is the same fact from the other side and names Auto Erase as the Pencil's own compensation for it.

## Color Replacement Tool

Substitutes one color for another in a stroke while keeping the luminosity and the texture underneath. Adobe describes the default options bar as one that "maintain[s] the luminosity and saturation of a color while replacing its hue."

**The whole tool is a blend mode.** Adobe: "Generally, you should keep the blending mode set to Color." That is why it is a replacement and not a paint — the mode transfers the source color's hue and chroma onto the destination's luminosity rather than compositing on top. `Hue`, `Saturation` and `Luminosity` transfer a different single component each. Remove the mode and the tool is a brush. The lead citation for this used to be a `helpx.adobe.com` URL that answers 403 to any fetch, which is not a citation; `sources` now leads with a page that is actually readable and carries the same claim — photoshopessentials.com's Color Replacement tutorial states "The default blend mode is Color," names the four modes as Hue, Saturation, Color and Luminosity, and gives each one's effect ("Color is the default blend mode and will change both the hue and saturation. The brightness will remain unchanged"). The Adobe CS4 manual mirror is retained for the "keep the blending mode set to Color" sentence, and its step sequence — brush tip, then Mode, then Sampling, Limits, Tolerance, Anti-aliased — is the bar order the table below uses.

**Sampling and Limits are two different questions.** Sampling decides _which_ color is the target: Continuous resamples under the crosshair on every move, Once locks the color of the first click for the whole stroke, Background Swatch targets the background color. Limits decides _how far_ the replacement spreads: Contiguous from the pixel under the pointer, Discontiguous anywhere under the pointer, Find Edges contiguous but stopping at strong shape edges. A tolerance slider then sets how similar a pixel must be to count. **The three lists are Adobe's and no Adobe page states which entry in either is the default**, so the table's Default column below reads "not stated" for both. Two third-party walks of the current tool agree on the defaults — glensmith: "Contiguous, which is the default option for the tool"; creativebloq: "They are set to Continuous by default" for Sampling — and `paint.ts` now attributes those inline as third-party rather than leaving the question open on the page.

**There is no top-bar `Size` on this tool, and the previous version of this file had one as a row.** Adobe's CS4 manual opens the tool with "Choose a brush tip in the options bar" and its six-step sequence never names a Size; creativebloq describes size, hardness and spacing as coming from the flyout ("you can make adjustments to the functionality of the Color Replacement tool. This includes the ability to tweak the size, hardness and spacing of the brush. Just head to the flyout menu"), and glensmith's own how-to step is "Adjust brush size. Select the Color Presets button on the Options bar", pointing at the picker popout rather than a slider. **So the 1–5000 figure is now inside the `Brush Preset Picker` detail, exactly as it is on the Brush and the Pencil**, and the app's top-bar `Size` slider (the `color-replacement-size` input in `ColorReplacement.tsx`, capped at 200 px) is recorded in `appNotes` as the app's own addition — which is where `types.ts` puts a control Photoshop's bar does not carry, and the same treatment the Brush's own top-bar Size already got.

**Two controls were added this pass, and both rest on one current source.** glensmith's Color Replacement page walks the bar past Anti-alias to two more entries it photographs: `Brush Angle` (reusing the Brush page's screenshot of the same widget) and `Pressure Sensitive Size`. Adobe's CS3/CS4 manuals stop at Anti-aliased. They are asserted as `missing` on one walkthrough each rather than on two, which `## Unverified` records, and the same two rows were added to the Eraser and the Clone Stamp, where glensmith is again the only witness.

**Limits is about reach, not membership, and that is what makes the `Contiguous` half of `purpose` right.** The `purpose` text used to contrast the tool with the Paint Bucket as replacing "as you drag rather than flooding a whole connected region in one click", which implied it does not flood. It does: Adobe's own wording for Contiguous is "Replaces colors that are contiguous with the color immediately under the pointer", and the app's code is more explicit still — it gathers every colour-matching pixel the stroke reached and floods outward from each one, one `createFloodFillMask` call per region (the `seedIndexes` loop inside `createColorReplacementPlan()` in `src/tools/colorReplacement.ts`). **What differs from the bucket is the trigger, not the reach**: one click chooses the colour to replace, and with Contiguous the replacement still takes the whole connected region from that one click. Discontiguous is the setting that confines it to the pixels under the brush. The mirror sentence in the Paint Bucket's `purpose` was half-false in the same way and has been corrected to match.

**Version note on Tolerance.** Adobe's CS4 manual says "enter a percentage value (ranging from 0 to 255)," which is the same odd unit the bucket uses, and the CS3 manual says the identical sentence — two Adobe manuals, one mirror each, so the range is as well sourced as anything in this file. The commonly-cited default of 30% comes from third parties (photoshopessentials.com), not from Adobe — see `## Unverified`. The app-side conflict is worth restating because it is a live disagreement inside one repository: the `Tolerance` slider in `ColorReplacement.tsx` caps at `COLOR_REPLACEMENT_TOLERANCE_MAX`, which is 100, and the component's comment claims "Adobe's tolerance scale for this tool is 0-100" — while Adobe's own manuals say 0 to 255 for this tool. The row is therefore `partial` in the app's favour: the control is wired and real, on a scale Adobe does not document for it.

**The bar is a real options bar, and every earlier status in this file was measured against a component that no longer exists.** The previous version of this section was built on `placeholderToolGroups['color-replacement']` and on the chip list in `toolbarPlaceholderConfig.ts`, and it claimed six rows were `inert` because six chips rendered. **Both of those are now gone**: the `color-replacement` entry of `placeholderToolGroups` in `toolbarPlaceholderConfig.ts` is a comment saying the tool "used to sit here", and the tool's `toolboxComponent` in `toolsMap.ts` is the real `ColorReplacement` component. - **`Brush Preset Picker`, `Mode` and `Anti-aliased` are `missing`**, not `inert`. Nothing renders any of them. The old note's careful sub-claim — that the `Mode` chip read `Blend Mode` and the `Anti-aliased` chip read `Anti-alias`, so those two were `inert` on a nearly-correct label rather than on an exact one — described a card that has since been deleted, and it is withdrawn with the card.
- **`Size` is `partial`, `Opacity` is `present`.** Both are live sliders, `Size` capped at 200 px where the picker offers 5000, and `Opacity` writing `args.alpha`. Both were `missing` before, which was the more serious error of the two kinds: it told a reader the tool cannot be scaled or softened when it can. **`Size` has since left `options[]` for a different reason, not a different finding**: Photoshop's bar does not carry it, so per the rule the Brush and Pencil follow it now lives in the `Brush Preset Picker` detail and in `appNotes`, and the app gap is unchanged.
- **`Limits` and `Tolerance` are `partial`.** Both are live and both diverge: the Limits list is two of Adobe's three entries with the second misspelled, and Tolerance is on a 0–100 scale against Adobe's documented 0–255. That is precisely what `partial` is for — wired, and wrong.
- **`Sampling` stays `inert`, but for the right reason.** The component does render a real `disabled` select, so `inert` is the honest status. What the old note got wrong was the reason: it said the chip named Adobe's control and nothing read it. In fact the select's options are `Current Layer` and `All Layers` — **neither of Adobe's Continuous / Once / Background Swatch**. The one control that decides _which colour is the target_ is therefore not merely unwired, it is wired to a different question entirely, and the app has no background color for Background Swatch to name.

**`Smoothness` is on this bar and is not a Photoshop control, so it is in `appNotes` and not in the table.** The `Smoothness` slider in `ColorReplacement.tsx` — written by `handleSmoothnessChange`, capped by `COLOR_REPLACEMENT_SMOOTHNESS_MAX` — renders 0-50, and the component's own comment says what it is — "Photoshop's Smoothness (Anti-aliased on older builds)". No Adobe page for this tool names a `Smoothness` control: the CS4 manual's step sequence ends at Anti-aliased, and the tool's option list in the manual and in the flylib transcription of the same manual both stop there. `types.ts` reserves `options[]` for controls Photoshop places in the bar, so a control Photoshop does not have does not belong in that array even when the app renders it prominently; it is recorded in `appNotes` instead, where an app-side addition belongs.

**The tool substitutes colour and preserves luminance, which is the whole of its contract and was the thing the previous version got most wrong.** `replaceColorPreservingLuminance()` in `src/tools/colorReplacement.ts` applies a single luminance **offset**, `out = replacement + (L − L_replacement)`, so each replaced pixel keeps its own shading and only its hue and chroma move. The alternative the function's own doc comment rules out is a scale, `replacement × (L / L_replacement)`, which is exact only while the result is in gamut: above the boundary it overruns 255 on its peak channel and a hue-preserving downscale lands every brighter pixel on the same colour, so the region flattens into one solid — the exact failure the function exists to prevent. There is no spray and no forced black anywhere in this tool.

**The colour is the store's foreground, read live, and the bar writes no colour at all.** `handlePointerUp` in `src/components/Main/ColorReplacementToolArea.tsx` builds the commit args as `{ ...args, color: store.color, points }` at pointer-up, for the reason the tool area states: the user can change the foreground while this tool stays selected, and a value captured when the bar mounted would replace with the colour that was current at the time. The options bar therefore renders the foreground as a **readout** (the "Sampled … / Replace with …" block in `ColorReplacement.tsx`) beside the sampled colour, and patches Tolerance, Smoothness, Size, Opacity and Limits but never a colour. The old note's claim that "only two components in the codebase ever write a paint tool's `args.color` from the store's foreground" was true when written and is now a misleading reason to distrust the result: this tool is a third such component, and it is the one that matters.

**It edits the active layer in place and never creates one.** `updateLayerByTool`, called from `handlePointerUp` in `src/components/Main/ColorReplacementToolArea.tsx`, the same distinction `EraserToolArea` draws, and for the same reason: a new transparent layer holding a recolouring composites as nothing. A drag that matched nothing writes no history at all — the plan is computed on the same pixels, with the same predicate and the same settings the processor will use, and a null plan ends the gesture silently (the `createColorReplacementPlan` / `if (!plan) return` guard in that same `handlePointerUp`), which is the rule `FillToolArea` already follows for a fill that matched nothing.

**There is no live preview, and that is a deliberate, documented divergence.** Photoshop repaints the replaced pixels live on the overlay as the stroke moves. The app draws no overlay for the tool at all, for the reason its own tool area gives: an honest overlay needs the full-layer plan recomputed on every pointer move, which is a multi-megapixel tolerance scan plus a flood per move, and a cheaper overlay that painted the brush swath regardless of tolerance "would show colour over pixels the commit is about to leave alone, which is a worse lie than none."

**Keyboard, and the `## Unverified` entry that said no source existed was wrong in the direction nobody suspected.** It recorded that "No source stating the letter was opened" and speculated `Shift+S`. **Four sources say otherwise, and one of them is Adobe's own document.** Adobe's CS4 quick-reference sheet prints "Brush/Pencil/Color Replacement Tool — B" as one row. photoshopessentials.com's Tools and Toolbar Overview lists "Brush Tool \* ( B )" and then "Color Replacement Tool ( B )", and explains the convention it is using: "The letter in parenthesis is the tool's keyboard shortcut. To cycle through tools with the same keyboard shortcut, press and hold Shift as you press the letter." cheat-sheet.org and learnthatyourself.com print the same collapsed row — "Brush Tool, Pencil Tool, Color Replacement tool, Mixer Brush Tool | B". The lone dissenter is Adobe's own _Photoshop CS Savvy_, which prints "Color Replacement (J)" — and `J` is the Healing family's letter in every other list opened, including Adobe's own, so it is a transcription artefact rather than a second reading.

**So the row is `B`**, phrased as what it actually is — the slot the tool shares with the Brush, the Pencil and the Mixer Brush, where the tile's default letter selects and `Shift+B` cycles — and the app already matched it, because `brushGroup` in `AppKeyboardShortcuts.tsx` contains `color-replacement`. The `J` outlier is recorded in `## Unverified` rather than hidden.

**Bar order, and the previous version of this note got it half wrong by omission.** Adobe's own Eraser page enumerates its bar as the `Mode` dropdown and then "In Brush and Pencil modes, adjust Size, Hardness, Opacity, and Flow from the Options bar" — so Mode precedes those four, and it is the only ordering evidence Adobe gives. glensmith's current Eraser page walks the whole bar left to right and fills the gap: Tool Presets, Brush Presets, Brush Settings panel, Mode, Opacity, Pressure Sensitive Opacity, Flow, Airbrush Effect, Smoothing, Smoothing Options, Brush Angle, Erase to History, Pressure Sensitive Size, Symmetry Options. **That is the table's order**, and it adds seven controls the previous version did not have at all — the Tool Preset Picker, the Brush Settings panel toggle, `Always Use Pressure for Opacity`, `Smoothing`, `Smoothing Options`, `Brush Angle` and `Always Use Pressure for Size`. Every one of the seven is `missing` in the app.

**What makes the Eraser a genuine exception rather than a copy of the Brush is that it keeps top-bar Size and Hardness anyway**, which is why a reader had no way to tell the two rules apart from the old file. Their position is the one piece of this order that is still inference rather than observation: Adobe enumerates them inside the sentence about the other four controls without placing them individually, and glensmith's walkthrough gives them no separate heading. Mode → Size → Hardness → Opacity → Flow is the order the table uses, on the strength of Adobe naming them in exactly that sequence.

**`Flow` is the finding on this tool.** The bar renders Photoshop's real `Flow` label over a 1–100 range and a hardcoded 100% value — and the `<input>` carries `disabled`, so nothing is ever written. There is no `flow` field on `EraserToolArgs` at all, which means the control is not merely unwired: it has no destination. The reason is structural, and the component says so: Flow is a per-dab alpha ramp, and this renderer issues one `beginPath` / `moveTo` / `stroke()` per stroke, which has no falloff and therefore no place to apply a per-dab rate. `Opacity` is the honest substitute, and it is what the app's Opacity actually is.

**The 1–100 range is the app's, not Adobe's, and the previous version of this note called it Photoshop's.** The component's own comment claims "Photoshop's 1-100", and the bar does render `min="1"` for `Flow` while `Opacity` renders `min="0"` (the `disabled` Flow input against the Opacity input, both in `Eraser.tsx`) — so the two controls on the same bar disagree about whether 0 is reachable. **Adobe's current page states the range and the default and says nothing about the minimum**; nothing in this pass found a page that does. The `Flow` detail in `paint.ts` now says exactly that and asserts no minimum, and the bound is in `## Unverified`.

**`Mode: Brush` and `Hardness` are both live now, and the previous version of this note filed them as missing capabilities that were structurally impossible. That is no longer true.** The Eraser has its own tip engine — `src/util/brush-engine.ts`, with `brushDabCenters`, `tipAlphaAt`, `stampBrushDab` and `clampBrushTipSettings` — and `eraser()` in `src/tools/eraser.ts` dispatches `mode === 'brush'` to `eraseWithTip` rather than to the single `stroke()` path. `Eraser.tsx` therefore offers Brush, Pencil and Block with **no `disabled` attribute on any option** (the three `<option>`s of the `eraser-mode` `<select>`), `handleHardnessChange` writes `hardness`, and the bar renders two live sliders in Brush mode: `Hardness` and `Spacing`. `buildTipSettings()` in `eraser.ts` clamps the incoming hardness, and each dab removes `alpha * (1 - weight)` where the weight is the tip's falloff, so a soft erase is a real erase.

So `Mode` moved `partial` → `present` and `Hardness` moved `missing` → `partial`. **`Hardness` is `partial` and not `present` for one reason: the bar withholds it in Pencil mode.** Adobe's page says Size, Hardness, Opacity and Flow are adjustable "In Brush and Pencil modes", so Pencil mode should still show a Hardness control — fixed at 100, per glensmith ("Pencil Mode will not let you use anything other than 100% Hardness") — and the app renders nothing there at all. Its Pencil mode is hard-edged by construction instead, which is the same by-construction edge the Brush and the Pencil have.

**Two controls on this bar are the app's own additions and belong in `appNotes`, not in the table.** `Spacing` is one: it is wired to the tip engine's spacing (`buildTipSettings()` in `src/tools/eraser.ts` hands `spacing` to `clampBrushTipSettings`, and the `brushDabCenters` loop inside `eraseWithTip()` consumes it) and Adobe puts spacing in the Brush Settings panel, not on this bar — glensmith's Eraser page documents Size, Hardness and the tip choice inside the Brush Presets popout and never lists Spacing among the bar's controls. The mode-gated rendering of `Hardness` is the other, and it is not an addition so much as a narrowed version of a real one.

**What matches.** The Eraser edits the **active** layer in place by subtracting alpha (`composite: 'destination-out'`, written by `buildEraseArgs()` in `src/tools/eraser.ts` — not by the `extent()` bounds helper, which is what the previous version cited) rather than creating a layer, which is what Photoshop does and which the DrawToolArea path cannot do. One history state per stroke, committed on pointer-up (`handlePointerUp` in `src/components/Main/EraserToolArea.tsx`). Opacity is correctly removed from the bar in Block mode rather than greyed out — Adobe removes it, and the component matches.

**And one modifier row is honoured here that no other tool in the family honours.** `handlePointerDown` in `src/components/Main/EraserToolArea.tsx` reads `event.shiftKey` with an `anchorRef` set by a preceding plain click, which is Adobe's two-click straight-line gesture for the brush-based tools — glensmith's Brush page, "To paint a perfectly straight line, click once at the beginning, then press and hold Shift while you click again. A perfectly straight line will be painted between these two points. **This works with all brush-based tools.**" The Eraser's `Alt` row is not honoured: nothing reads `altKey` in that tool area, so the temporary Erase to History gesture Adobe documents there is absent. `ToolModifier` carries no `status`, so both facts are here and in the `appNotes` rather than on the rows.

**Keyboard.** `E` selects the Eraser, matching Photoshop, and Adobe's own book gives the family cycle explicitly: "To switch quickly among the Eraser, Magic Eraser, and Background Eraser tools, press Shift+E" (flylib, _How-To Hints_). The app does **not** bind `Shift+E` — the plain `e` `useKeyDownShortcut` registration in `AppKeyboardShortcuts.tsx` carries no Shift twin — so the Eraser, the Magic Eraser and the Background Eraser are a single app shortcut with no cycle among them. It also reaches the Eraser by cycling the Brush tile with `Shift+B`, which is its own choice; Photoshop keeps the Eraser in its own Tools-panel slot. `[`, `]`, `0` and `1` are bound for the Eraser through `tipSizeField` and `opacityField`, so its `shortcuts` array now carries all four.

## Magic Eraser Tool

The Paint Bucket run in reverse: one click erases every pixel within Tolerance of the clicked pixel. Adobe's own current page gives the bar in five lines, and the list is exact:

> In the Options bar, enter a value between 0 and 255 for Tolerance: Lower values erase colors similar to the selected pixel. Higher values erase a broad range of colors. Select Anti-alias to smooth the edges of the erased area. Select Contiguous to erase pixels of similar color adjacent to the selected pixel. Deselect Contiguous to erase all matching pixels across the layer. Select Sample All Layers to erase pixels based on colors from all visible layers or leave it unchecked to sample only from the active layer. Enter a value to set Opacity: 100% erases pixels. Lower values partially erase pixels, creating transparency.

**The control is `Anti-alias` and the previous version of this file had it as `Anti-aliased`, which is the older spelling.** The same tool's older Adobe page — `/using/tool-techniques/magic-eraser-tool.html`, last updated 2024-06-18 — says "Select Anti-aliased to smooth the edges", and the CS3 manual mirror says "Select Anti-aliased to smooth the edges of the area you erase." The **current** page, `/desktop/repair-retouch/clean-restore-images/change-similar-pixels-with-the-magic-eraser-tool.html`, last updated 2025-11-04, says "Select Anti-alias to smooth the edges of the erased area." Both URLs stay in `sources`, because the point is the version boundary and not the wording: the name moved. The Paint Bucket's row moved the same way — its 2024-05-08 page lists "Mode, Opacity, Tolerance, Anti-alias, Contiguous, and All Layers" and its current 2026-02-23 page says "Anti-alias: Create smoother edges on your filled areas", where the CS3 manual and the underwaterphotography mirror both read `Anti-aliased`. **The app renders the legacy spelling** in both bars, which is an app fact and now lives in the two `appNotes` rather than being a reason to keep Adobe's name wrong.

**Note the naming difference from the bucket, which is real and not a typo.** The bucket's checkbox is `All Layers` and the Magic Eraser's is `Sample All Layers`. They are the same idea — read the color data from the merged visible layers rather than the active one — and the two Adobe pages use the two different words.

**The cost of the tool is that there is no preview.** It works like the Magic Wand without the selection, so the region it is about to remove is visible only in the History panel afterwards. glensmith.co.uk makes the comparison plain: "with the Magic Wand you can see if any part of your subject will be selected and you can adjust this before deleting pixels. The Magic Eraser does not allow you to see this."

**Default Tolerance is 32, and 32 is third-party.** Adobe's current page gives the range and the direction and stops: "In the Options bar, enter a value between 0 and 255 for Tolerance: Lower values erase colors similar to the selected pixel. Higher values erase a broad range of colors." The 32 comes from two independent third parties — glensmith.co.uk ("The default setting is 32px and this works well for many scenarios") and an Adobe community answer from an Adobe Community Expert ("The default 32 is way to high for the background of your image—that's why it's eating away at the interior light colors"). The bar's scale of 0–255 is Adobe's, from the CS3 manual. `paint.ts` now attributes the default inline in the same form the Color Replacement tool's Tolerance row already used, rather than printing an unsourced `Default 32`.

**`Anti-alias` is `inert` and the reason is arithmetic.** It renders the app's own legacy `Anti-aliased` label and Photoshop's real checked state, and the DOM input is `disabled`. The flood fill's test is a hard threshold (`isPixelWithinTolerance` in `src/util/floodFill.ts`) producing a `Uint8Array` mask of 0s and 1s, so there is no coverage value to smooth — anti-aliasing needs a fractional boundary, and this tool never computes one.

**Tolerance is on the right scale, which is a real and easy thing to get wrong.** The Magic Wand uses 0–100 against a 4-channel distance that includes alpha; the bucket, the Magic Eraser and the Background Eraser use 0–255 against a 3-channel RGB distance, where a pixel differing only in alpha is _inside_ the region. The app gets this right, and the two scales cannot drift into each other because both bars render against the same shared constant.

**Version boundary — the Background layer rule is the Eraser's, not the Magic Eraser's, and the two tools disagree.** This is the one place where a current Photoshop claim and a pre-CS phrasing conflict outright, and the resolution is that they are describing **different tools**.

- **Eraser.** Adobe's current page (last updated 2026-02-23): "If erasing on the Background layer or a locked transparent layer, the erased area is filled with the background color." Adobe's CS3 manual, same rule: "If you're working in the background or in a layer with transparency locked, the pixels change to the background color; otherwise, the pixels are erased to transparency."
- **Magic Eraser.** Adobe's current page (last updated 2026-02-23): "If you select the background layer, Adobe Photoshop automatically converts it into a regular layer before erasing similar pixels" — so a Background layer is **not** a background-colour case; it is converted and then cleared to transparency. Adobe's CS3 manual says the same in one sentence: "If you're working in a layer with locked transparency, the pixels change to the background color. If you click in the background, it is converted to a layer and all similar pixels change to transparent."

An earlier version of this file asserted both behaviours in one sentence for the Magic Eraser, which is self-contradictory and was refuted by the very page it cited. The confusable pre-CS phrasing — "if you're working in the background, or in a layer with locked transparency, the pixels change to the background color" — is the **Eraser** rule, and it is the wording that survives to the present day on Adobe's Eraser page. Third-party prose blurs the pair further: dummies.com's Elements 9 walkthrough states the Magic Eraser's rule correctly ("Unless you're working on a layer with the transparency locked, the pixels are erased to transparency. If you're working on an image with just a background, Elements converts the background into a layer") while its adjacent sentence about the plain Eraser keeps the older conflation. The rule for this file is therefore: **locked transparency → background colour, on either tool; Background layer → background colour for the Eraser, auto-converted to a normal layer and cleared to transparency for the Magic Eraser.**

The app refuses the Bedrock layer outright and treats every other layer identically, so it diverges from Photoshop under either rule.

**Keyboard, and the `## Unverified` entry that said nothing was found has now been given something.** Adobe's own book states the family binding outright: "To switch quickly among the Eraser, Magic Eraser, and Background Eraser tools, press `Shift+E`" (flylib, _How-To Hints_). The same book confirms the family and that the Magic Eraser is its third member, and the CS2 Help Desk Book lists the Magic Eraser's controls as "Contiguous and Sample All Layers checkboxes, Tolerance, and Opacity" — the same five, independently. So the row is `Shift+E`, described as what it is: a cycle through a shared slot, not a letter of its own.

**Adobe's own help page still prints "(E)" for this tool**, which contradicts the convention that `E` belongs to the Eraser group; that is a version artefact of a page that lists the letter beside the tool name and has not been revised since the family cycle existed. It is kept in `sources` and named here rather than resolved, because a current Adobe page and an Adobe book disagree and the book's statement is about the behaviour rather than about the label.

The app binds no cycle at all: the plain `e` registration in `AppKeyboardShortcuts.tsx` is a single plain `e` with no Shift twin, and the `G`/`Shift+G` fill group contains the Paint Bucket and the Gradient only, so the Magic Eraser is reached from nowhere in the app's keyboard table.

## Paint Bucket Tool

Fills the whole connected region of similar color around the click in a single click. Shares a Tools-panel slot with the Gradient tool; Adobe's page says so and the `G` / `Shift+G` cycle follows from it.

**Adobe's bar, in their order**: Fill (Foreground or Pattern), then under Blending the Mode, then Opacity, then Tolerance, then Anti-alias, Contiguous and All Layers. Adobe's Tolerance note is the useful one: "Lower values (10-20) fill similar colors, while higher values (100 or more) fill a broader range," and "Lower opacity values create more transparent fills." Adobe gives the bucket's Tolerance range and gives no default, so the widely-cited 32 is third-party here exactly as it is on the Magic Eraser, and `paint.ts` attributes it inline.

**`Mode` here is the 27 layer blend modes plus `Clear`, not plus `Dissolve`, `Behind` and `Clear`.** Dissolve is one of the 27 — Adobe's own `BlendMode` enum has `DISSOLVE` in it — and `Behind` is the Brush's alone, working "only in layers with Lock Transparency deselected". `Clear` is the one this tool gets, alongside the shape tools, the Brush, the Pencil, the Fill command and the Stroke command. The old wording double-counted Dissolve on all three tools that carried it and gave the bucket a `Behind` it does not have; the Brush, the Pencil, the bucket and the Clone Stamp rows have all been corrected against Adobe's blend-mode descriptions page and the UXP constants reference.

**`Mode` is `missing` for a structural reason that is worth more than the fact itself.** The Paint Bucket is a `commit: 'raster'` tool that edits the **active** layer's pixels in place, so a blend mode here would be a per-pixel composite against a destination the processor does not address — unlike the Brush, where the stroke creates its own layer and a layer blend mode is exactly the right seam. Adding it needs both an args field and a different composite primitive.

**`Pattern` is `partial` rather than `missing` because half of it works.** The `Fill` control itself is real: the readout shows the store's foreground color live, so a user can see what the click will paint, and `Foreground` is the one fill the tool performs. The `Pattern` option is present with its real label and `disabled`.

**The app renders the legacy `Anti-aliased` label on this bar, and the table says `Anti-alias`.** The checkbox in the shared fill bar is hardcoded to the older spelling, which Adobe's current Paint Bucket page has dropped. The control is inert either way, so nothing about the app's behaviour changes; the divergence that matters is that a screenshot of this bar does not read the way Adobe's current page describes it, and that belongs in `appNotes` rather than in a control name.

**The one place this implementation is better than the obvious version.** The fill is a real source-over (the `blendPixel` call inside `paintBucket()` in `src/tools/fill.ts`), not a lerp of straight RGB against straight RGB. The difference is visible at partial opacity over a transparent region, where a lerp writes half the fill color mixed with half of nothing — half-black — and correct compositing writes half coverage of clean red. Adobe's own sentence, "Lower opacity values create more transparent fills," only holds if the fill color survives the blend.

**The commit is the click, with no drag.** `FillToolArea` handles `pointerdown` and nothing else, by design: Photoshop commits the fill on the click, so a drag-to-adjust gesture would add a second history state the product does not have. A fill that matches nothing writes no history at all — the mask is computed before the commit, and a null result ends the gesture silently.

**Keyboard.** `G` selects it, `Shift+G` cycles back from the Gradient. Matches Photoshop. The bucket is **not** in the `tipSizeField` or `opacityField` tables in the global keyboard file, which is right: it has no tip, and its Opacity is a fill strength rather than a brush ceiling. That is why its `shortcuts` array carries one row where the Eraser's carries five.

## Clone Stamp Tool

Copies pixels from one part of an image and paints them into another. Adobe's gesture, verbatim: "Press the Alt key (Windows) or the Option key (macOS) and click the area of the image that you want to copy. This sets a starting point from which the tool will begin copying. Release the Alt or Option key and drag over the new area where you want to place your copied content."

**Full bar, left to right** — `[Tool presets] | [Brush presets] | Mode ▾ | Opacity :100% | Flow :100% | [airbrush] | ⊙Pressure-for-opacity | ⊙Pressure-for-size | ☑Aligned | Sample ▾ | ☐Ignore Adjustment Layers | Brush Angle | [Clone Source panel toggle]`.

**Two rows were missing from this table and are now in it.** The first was the `Tool Preset Picker`, described inside a neighbouring row and drawn in the bar list but having no row of its own. The second is the **airbrush toggle**, which the previous bar list itself did not contain and which the table did not either: four independent sources put it on this tool's bar — phlearn, "The airbrush build-up option and the two pressure toggles (pressure for opacity, pressure for size) are all switched off in his setup"; enviragallery, "The Airbrush icon enables you to brush as you would with an airbrush to create buildups and layer your stamp tool"; glensmith's Clone Stamp page, "## Airbrush — Build up paint in the same way as a real world spray paint effect"; and printyourphotos's walkthrough, which counts the toggles to the right of Opacity. Adobe's current clone-stamp page enumerates only Mode, Opacity and Flow, so this is one of the two places where a current Adobe page is narrower than the bar — the same shape as the Brush's `Size`, and the reason the row's status is decided on the union rather than on that page.

`Brush Angle` joins them for the same reason as on the other four brush-family bars: glensmith's Clone Stamp page carries it, and no Adobe page for this tool names it. **The bar is thirteen controls, and ten of them are `missing`** — up from eleven and eight.

`Flow` and the airbrush semantics are the Brush tool's, already researched there, so they are not re-derived. `Sample` and `Ignore Adjustment Layers` are Adobe's CS4 manual verbatim, and the manual names the position of the second control, which is the part most third-party pages get wrong:

> Sample — Samples data from the layers you specify. To sample from the active layer and visible layers below it, choose Current And Below. To sample only from the active layer, choose Current Layer. To sample from all visible layers, choose All Layers. To sample from all visible layers **except adjustment layers**, choose All Layers and click the **Ignore Adjustment Layers icon to the right of the Sample pop-up menu**.

The button was added in CS3 as an unlabeled toggle, per properproof's write-up: "Adobe Photoshop CS3 includes a small improvement… a new, unlabeled button that lets you ignore all adjustment layers while cloning," motivated by a concrete failure — "If we start using our clone tool with the color adjustment layer visible, and our clone tool set to 'Sample All Layers' we end up cloning with a weird green hue." Four independent sources place it immediately right of the Sample menu: two mirrors of the CS4 manual, jkost, and underwaterphotoshop.

**Aligned is two different rules, and this is where the app is wrong.** Adobe's own clone-stamp page states the pair, and it is the stroke-to-stroke rule verbatim: "Select **Aligned to paint the most recent sampling point. Deselect Aligned to restart sampling from the original point each time.**" That sentence alone establishes the anchor changes on every stroke when Aligned is on and does not when it is off, which is the whole of the rule the app diverges from, and it is the citation this should rest on.

The **mechanism** behind "the most recent sampling point" is stated more explicitly only by uidaho's tool-bar page — a 1990s course reference, not Adobe, and now returning 403 to a direct fetch, so it is retained as corroboration and labelled as what it is rather than leading the paragraph:

> If you have Aligned checked, your clones will be applied as if you were working with **a copy of the image that has been displaced the distance of your first stroke after Alt-clicking**. All subsequent strokes (prior to another Alt-click) will fill in the copied image relative to that first stroke. When you do not have Aligned checked, all (separate) strokes after an Alt-click will apply **from the single spot that you Alt-clicked**. Each time you end one stroke and make a new one, the new one will use the same starting spot.

So the anchor is established **once, at the first stroke after the Alt-click, and persists across strokes** until another Alt-click replaces it. Aligned and unaligned are the same subtraction against two different anchors: a stored one versus the fixed Alt-clicked point. The same page states the unaligned consequence for the reader directly: "Thus you can repeatedly apply the same image data (the Alt-click location) to different spots."

**The app implements neither arm.** `cloneOffset()` in `src/tools/cloneStamp.ts` takes the **current gesture's** path and anchors to `points[0]` when aligned, `points[points.length - 1]` when not. There is no field anywhere on `CloneStampToolArgs` for a stored offset. Consequences:

- _Aligned_: the offset is constant within one stroke — which matches — and re-anchors to each new stroke's own start, which is precisely the drift the mode exists to remove. The control is wired and reads as its own value; it is `partial`, not `present`.
- _Unaligned_: the read point slides backwards by the drag's displacement as the gesture progresses, rather than staying at the Alt-clicked spot with only the destination moving.

Both arms are one subtraction, so this is a **missing anchor, not a wrong formula** — the fix is a stored offset on the args, not new pixel code. This is the single most consequential finding on this tool.

**The source-not-output guarantee is real, and it holds for a structural reason worth naming twice.** Every read in `cloneDab` indexes an immutable copy of the raster and every write indexes the buffer, so no dab can sample a texel a previous dab laid down. The copy is taken once per _processor invocation_, and the guarantee upgrades to once per _stroke_ for a second reason: the live preview re-runs the processor on every pointer move but always against the store's own pre-stroke raster, never the previous frame's preview — `tempUpdateLayer()` in `src/contexts/useLayer.ts` reads `layer.tmpCanvas ?? layer.bitmap` from the store layer (the previous version of this note cited `tempUpdateLayer`'s trapezoid-transform preview branch instead, whose preview layer is built with `tmpCanvas: null`), while the accumulated preview lives in `previewLayersRef` and is never the input. The commit then re-runs the processor once over the whole path before publishing. So neither the live preview nor the committed stroke can compound itself.

**The Photoshop side of that is not a documented guarantee, and the honest record says so.** See `## Unverified`: the widely repeated "Photoshop samples the source once at stroke start" rule is folklore with no Adobe source. What _is_ sourced is the artifact and its documented remedy — the classic failure being that the source cursor reaching the area already painted makes the removed thing reappear, with the advice to "clone-stamp a little bit at a time… stamp a little, release, stamp a little, release" (graphicdesign.stackexchange). That the remedy is needed is itself the evidence that a per-stroke snapshot is not something Photoshop can be relied on to provide, which is what makes the app's structural version worth stating as a divergence in its favour.

**Layer edge cases.** Adobe states one directly: "Make sure you are not working on an adjustment layer. The Clone Stamp tool does not work on adjustment layers."

**The lock gap this section used to report is closed, and it was closed centrally rather than in this tool.** Two previous versions of this note claimed the `isEnabled` gate in `CloneStampToolArea.tsx` "does not check the layer's lock flag", so a locked layer would accept a cloned stroke that PSD then protected from every other reader. **Both claims are now false.** `updateLayerByTool()` in `src/contexts/useLayer.ts` returns early on `isLayerLocked(layers[id])`, and `tempUpdateLayer()` does the same on `isLayerLocked(layer)` — and `tempUpdateLayer` is the path the live preview and the clone-source write both take, so neither can reach a locked layer either. The guard is `isLayerLocked(layer)`, which is the one predicate the store applies everywhere.

What survives is the narrower, verified fact that was already in the third version of this paragraph and is still true. `layer.locked` is declared on the `Layer` type in `src/contexts/layer-types.ts` and is set true in exactly one place: the PSD import walk (`visit()` inside `loadPsd()` in `src/contexts/psd-utils.ts` folds a child's own lock into its parent's and writes the result onto the layer). Every other construction path writes `false` — a new layer from `createLayerSource()` in `src/contexts/layer-utils.ts`, the composite fallback layer built in the same `loadPsd()`, a materialized document layer from `sourceToRuntimeLayer()` in `src/contexts/history-adapter.ts` (not the selection-guard comment inside `legacyHistoryToDurable()`, which the previous version cited). There is no lock control in any panel and no lock icon anywhere in the UI, so a user cannot set one and cannot see one. So: an imported-file fact, not a live-UI one, and not a defect in the Clone Stamp.

`Ignore Adjustment Layers` has nothing to ignore here even in principle: a layer source in this app is `blank`, `image`, `tool` or an `affine-transform` operation, and Levels, Color, Transform and Blur are destructive operations on a raster rather than layers sitting above a clone target.

**The Clone Source panel** holds up to five sampling sources, "these can be from the same document or from multiple documents" (photoshopcafe), with per-source flip, rotate, scale and opacity, a Show Overlay checkbox, and a Clipped option that limits the overlay to the brush size. Since CS4 the sampled source is displayed live under the brush, which hoffmanartdesign credits to a feature "migrated over from the Vanishing Point filter" and describes as "a colored blob that is the source of confusion for many people." None of it exists in the app; the only trace of the idea is a crosshair drawn on the overlay canvas.

**What matches.** The Alt-click sets the source at **pointer-down** and paints nothing; the decision is made at down, so a drag that began as a plain press keeps cloning even if the modifier is released mid-stroke, and a press that began with it down never starts a stroke at all. Both the source and the stroke are rebased into the active layer's frame, which is safe precisely because the processor only uses their _difference_. With no source set the gesture is **refused** — not-allowed cursor, no stroke, no history entry — rather than accepted and committed as a no-op. The copy carries the source texel's own alpha, so cloning out of a transparent region leaves the destination transparent instead of painting it opaque black. Size, Hardness and Opacity are all wired, and the tip is borrowed from the shared brush engine, so a soft Clone edge is the same soft edge.

**Keyboard.** `S` selects it, matching Photoshop, and `Shift+S` cycles to the Pattern Stamp, which is Photoshop's convention for a shared slot. `[`, `]`, `0` and `1` are bound for the Clone Stamp through `tipSizeField` and `opacityField` — it is in both tables, keyed to `size` and `alpha` respectively — so its `shortcuts` array now carries all four where it previously carried one.

## Spot Healing Brush Tool

Adobe documents Spot Healing as a click-or-drag retouch tool with Content-Aware,
Proximity Match and Create Texture modes, plus Diffusion and Sample All Layers
controls. Adobe does not publish the Content-Aware or Create Texture algorithm,
so this app implements only a deterministic Proximity Match approximation: each
brush dab samples an outward ring from the active layer and blends that local
texture through the configured size, hardness and opacity. The processor is a
replayable `raster-processor` action, and one stroke creates one history item.

The current MVP deliberately omits Content-Aware, Create Texture, Diffusion,
Sample All Layers, pressure-driven size and blend-mode selection. Those controls
remain `missing` in `paint.ts` rather than appearing as dead options. Photoshop's
J shortcut is represented by the app's Spot Healing selection; the other J-family
retouch tools are not yet registered for cycling.

## Healing Brush Tool

Adobe distinguishes the Healing Brush from Spot Healing by its manual
Alt/Option-click source. This implementation samples a source point, carries the
source offset across the stroke when Aligned is on, and adjusts the sampled RGB
texture toward the destination luminance before compositing it through the tip.
That is a deterministic approximation, not a claim to Adobe’s private
Poisson-blending implementation.

Pattern source, Sample Current & Below / All Layers, Ignore Adjustment Layers,
Diffusion, pressure-driven size, blend-mode selection, cross-document sampling
and selection clipping remain deferred. They are recorded as missing controls in
`paint.ts` rather than exposed as inert options.
